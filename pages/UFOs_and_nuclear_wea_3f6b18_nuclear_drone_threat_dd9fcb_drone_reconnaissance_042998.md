## Introduction

Small drones create a [nuclear security]({{ 'security-concerns-0719dd/' | relative_url }}) problem not only because they can carry equipment or interfere with operations, but because they can observe. A commercially available unmanned aircraft can approach restricted airspace, record images, map visible features, and reveal details about how a nuclear facility is arranged and protected. For security planners, this reconnaissance function matters because information gathered from outside a site can reduce uncertainty for anyone trying to understand access points, surveillance coverage, response patterns, or operational routines.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nrc.gov/reading-rm/doc-collections/fact-sheets/fs-drone-pwr-plant-security" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nrc.gov">[Nuclear Regulatory Commission]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nrc.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Nuclear Regulatory CommissionDrones and Nuclear Power Plant Security &#124; Nuclear Regulatory CommissionJanuary 21, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 21, 2025</span></span></span>

The concern is not that every drone near a nuclear site represents a deliberate intelligence operation. Many incidents involve hobbyists, mistaken flights, or activity with unclear intent. The security challenge is that observation itself can be valuable, and a site operator may have limited time to determine whether a drone is harmless or gathering information. This has pushed nuclear authorities towards stronger detection, reporting, and counter-drone measures designed to identify and assess unauthorised flights before they become more serious threats.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nrc.gov/reading-rm/doc-collections/fact-sheets/fs-drone-pwr-plant-security" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nrc.gov">[Nuclear Regulatory Commission]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nrc.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Nuclear Regulatory CommissionDrones and Nuclear Power Plant Security &#124; Nuclear Regulatory CommissionJanuary 21, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 21, 2025</span></span></span>

## Information gathered by small drones

### Aerial views reveal what ground observers cannot

A drone gives an observer a temporary aerial perspective over a facility. Unlike someone viewing a nuclear site from a public road or nearby land, a drone can move above obstacles and capture views of areas that are difficult to see from ground level. Images and video may reveal broad features such as building placement, perimeter arrangements, construction work, vehicle movement patterns, or the relationship between different parts of a complex.

For nuclear facilities, the value of this information comes from accumulation. A single image may appear insignificant, but repeated flights can build a more complete picture of a site. Changes over time — such as temporary barriers, maintenance activity, or altered traffic patterns — may be visible from the air even when they are not publicly announced.

Security agencies therefore treat drone observation as an information risk even when no physical intrusion occurs. The U.S. Nuclear Regulatory Commission (NRC) notes that reported drone sightings over nuclear power plants are shared with federal and law-enforcement partners because they may require assessment as part of wider security monitoring.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nrc.gov/reading-rm/doc-collections/fact-sheets/fs-drone-pwr-plant-security" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nrc.gov">[Nuclear Regulatory Commission]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nrc.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Nuclear Regulatory CommissionDrones and Nuclear Power Plant Security &#124; Nuclear Regulatory CommissionJanuary 21, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 21, 2025</span></span></span>

### Reconnaissance does not require advanced equipment

The threat comes partly from the accessibility of modern consumer drones. Many small systems are equipped with high-resolution cameras, stabilisation technology, and navigation features that allow controlled flight close to infrastructure. Research into protecting restricted airspace has highlighted that drone detection is difficult because small aircraft can be hard to distinguish from background objects and may require combined radar, optical, acoustic, and radio-frequency monitoring approaches.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2111.03760" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: arxiv.org">[arXiv]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">arxiv.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">arXiv Securing your Airspace: Detection of Drones Trespassing Protected Areas</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Securing your Airspace: Detection of Drones Trespassing Protected AreasNovember 5, 2021...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 5, 2021</span></span></span>

This creates a mismatch between the simplicity of gathering information and the complexity of defending against it. A person does not necessarily need specialised military equipment to collect useful visual data. The security challenge is identifying when ordinary-looking activity becomes a deliberate attempt to understand a protected location.

## Security weaknesses revealed by observation

### Drones can test the limits of surveillance

Repeated drone flights may reveal more than the facility itself. They can expose how quickly security personnel notice an aircraft, which detection systems are effective, and how operators respond to an unfamiliar object. In security terms, the response process can be as important as the information collected from the facility.

This is one reason nuclear security programmes focus on detection and assessment rather than assuming intent from the first sighting. The National Nuclear Security Administration (NNSA), which protects sensitive nuclear security facilities in the United States, describes its counter-unmanned aircraft programme as involving detection, tracking, assessment, and response to unauthorised drone activity.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.energy.gov/nnsa/protecting-nnsa-facilities-counter-unmanned-aircraft-systems" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: energy.gov">[The Department of Energy&#x27;s Energy.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">energy.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on energy.gov.</span></span></span>

Observation can also create uncertainty during real incidents. Security teams must decide whether a drone is collecting information, conducting accidental photography, or preparing for a different type of activity. That uncertainty consumes attention and resources, particularly at facilities where security procedures must account for a wide range of possible threats.

### The location of a drone can matter as much as its payload

A common misunderstanding is that a drone is only dangerous if it carries an object. Around nuclear facilities, proximity and access to restricted views are themselves security concerns. A drone that never touches a building may still provide intelligence about the environment around it.

The NRC has emphasised that commercial nuclear plants already use layered security measures, including physical barriers, surveillance systems, and trained security forces. The drone issue adds another layer because it involves controlling information exposure in airspace that was not traditionally viewed as an easy route for observation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nrc.gov/reading-rm/doc-collections/fact-sheets/fs-drone-pwr-plant-security" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nrc.gov">[Nuclear Regulatory Commission]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nrc.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Nuclear Regulatory CommissionDrones and Nuclear Power Plant Security &#124; Nuclear Regulatory CommissionJanuary 21, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 21, 2025</span></span></span>

## Protecting sensitive facility layouts

### Counter-drone systems focus on early detection

Protecting against reconnaissance begins with knowing that a drone is present. Nuclear security organisations increasingly use counter-unmanned aircraft systems, commonly called counter-UAS or CUAS, to detect and track unauthorised flights. These systems may combine different technologies because no single sensor works reliably in every environment.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.energy.gov/nnsa/protecting-nnsa-facilities-counter-unmanned-aircraft-systems" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: energy.gov">[The Department of Energy&#x27;s Energy.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">energy.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on energy.gov.</span></span></span>

NNSA facilities provide an example of this approach. The agency states that its CUAS programme is designed to detect, monitor, assess, and respond to unauthorised drone activity at sensitive sites. Individual facilities, including the Y-12 National Security Complex and the Pantex Plant, have deployed systems intended to detect, identify, and track potentially malicious drones.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.energy.gov/nnsa/protecting-nnsa-facilities-counter-unmanned-aircraft-systems" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: energy.gov">[energy.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">energy.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on energy.gov.</span></span></span>

The aim is not simply to remove a drone from the air. Security teams need information about the event itself: where the drone came from, how it was operating, and whether the activity indicates a wider concern.

### Limiting information exposure is part of modern nuclear security

Traditional nuclear security has focused heavily on preventing unauthorised access, sabotage, and theft of nuclear material. Drone reconnaissance adds a different dimension: protecting knowledge about the site. A facility can remain physically secure while still facing risks if outsiders gain a clearer understanding of its layout or security practices.

This issue connects drone activity with the broader discussion of unidentified aerial activity near nuclear sites. Historical reports of unknown objects often centred on uncertainty about what was observed, while modern drone cases introduce a more practical security question: whether an identifiable technology is being used to collect information. The distinction matters because responses can be based on known capabilities rather than speculation.

The most documented high-profile cases have occurred in conflict environments. At Ukraine’s Zaporizhzhia Nuclear Power Plant, the International Atomic Energy Agency (IAEA) has repeatedly warned about drone activity near the facility, including incidents affecting buildings near the plant. The agency’s concern has focused on nuclear safety and security risks created by military activity and the possibility that continued drone operations could escalate dangers around a sensitive site.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.world-nuclear-news.org/articles/iaea-warning-over-drones-near-nuclear-sites" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: world-nuclear-news.org">[World Nuclear News]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">world-nuclear-news.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">World Nuclear News IAEA warning over drones near nuclear sites</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">World Nuclear NewsIAEA warning over drones near nuclear sites - World Nuclear NewsMay 22, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 22, 2025</span></span></span>

For nuclear facilities outside active conflict zones, the main lesson is narrower but important: a drone does not need to cause damage to create a security problem. The ability to see, record, and learn about a protected facility means that controlling unauthorised aerial observation has become part of the modern nuclear security challenge.
