## Introduction

Military infrared videos are among the most influential pieces of modern UAP evidence, but they are also among the easiest to overinterpret. Recordings from fighter aircraft targeting pods can show real objects and genuine [sensor data]({{ 'sensor-data/' | relative_url }}) while still failing to answer the questions investigators most want to ask: how far away was the object, how large was it, how fast was it moving, and was its apparent behaviour caused by the object or by the observing system? NASA’s UAP independent study concluded that many unresolved cases remain difficult not because there is no information, but because observations often lack the calibration, multiple measurements, and sensor metadata needed for rigorous analysis.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nasa.gov/news-release/update-nasa-shares-uap-independent-study-report-names-director/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nasa.gov">[NASA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nasa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">UPDATE: NASA Shares UAP Independent Study Report; Names Director</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UPDATE: NASA Shares UAP Independent Study Report; Names Director - NASASeptember 14, 2023...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: September 14, 2023</span></span></span>

The Navy FLIR, Gimbal, and GoFast videos demonstrate this problem clearly. Their importance is not that they prove extraordinary technology or that they are meaningless. Rather, they show how a short infrared clip can become a difficult scientific puzzle when recorded by a sensor designed for military operations rather than open investigation. Understanding the limits of infrared imaging is therefore essential when evaluating UAP reports, including those connected to broader questions about military security and nuclear-related incidents.

## How FLIR systems record unusual objects

Forward Looking Infrared (FLIR) systems detect infrared radiation, usually associated with heat, rather than producing a normal visible-light photograph. Military targeting pods combine infrared sensors with optics, stabilisation systems, tracking software, and aircraft data to help pilots identify and engage targets. They are extremely capable tools, but their purpose is operational decision-making rather than producing a complete scientific measurement of an unknown object.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.af.mil/About-Us/Fact-Sheets/Display/Article/104571/litening-advance-targeting/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: af.mil">[Air Force]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">af.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Air Force LITENING ADVANCE TARGETING &gt; Air Force &gt; Fact Sheet Display</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Air Force LITENING ADVANCE TARGETING &gt; Air Force &gt; Fact Sheet Display</span></span></span>

An infrared image can reveal that something produces a detectable thermal contrast against the background, but it does not automatically reveal what that thing is. A bright spot might represent a hot engine component, reflected infrared energy, atmospheric effects, a distant aircraft, or a small object close to the camera. Without additional information, the image alone often cannot determine the object’s physical characteristics.

Several missing measurements are especially important:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **Distance:** A small object nearby and a large object far away can create similar images.
* **Size:** Infrared videos usually show angular size rather than true dimensions.
* **Temperature:** A bright infrared signature does not directly translate into a simple temperature reading because emissivity, atmospheric absorption, and sensor processing affect the image.
* **Motion:** Apparent movement depends on both the object and the aircraft carrying the camera.
* **Sensor processing:** Tracking modes, image enhancement, stabilisation, and display settings can alter how an object appears.

</div>

NASA highlighted the importance of metadata such as sensor type, calibration information, acquisition conditions, and instrument characteristics because without those details it is difficult to determine whether an unusual appearance comes from the object or the sensor system itself.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://smd-cms.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2023/09/uap-independent-study-team-final-report.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: smd-cms.nasa.gov">[NASA Science]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">smd-cms.nasa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Science NASA</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UNIDENTIFIED ANOMALOUS PHENOMENA Independent...</span></span></span>

This distinction matters because a FLIR recording is not simply a window looking at reality. It is the output of a chain: infrared radiation enters the optics, is converted by the detector, processed by onboard software, stabilised relative to the aircraft, and displayed to an operator. Each stage can affect interpretation.

## Perspective effects in apparent movement

One of the most common mistakes in analysing FLIR UAP videos is treating movement on the screen as direct evidence of physical movement through the sky. A camera mounted on a fast-moving aircraft creates a complicated viewing geometry. An object can appear to move rapidly across the image even when it is moving slowly, stationary, or simply being viewed from a changing angle.

The GoFast video is a major example of this issue. Released by the Department of Defense as one of the Navy UAP videos, it appeared to show a small object travelling extremely quickly over the ocean. AARO’s later analysis concluded that the object did not demonstrate anomalous performance and assessed that its apparent speed was strongly affected by viewing geometry, with the object estimated to be much higher and moving far more slowly than the initial impression suggested.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.aaro.mil/Portals/136/PDFs/case_resolution_reports/AARO_GoFast_Case_Resolution_Card_Methodology_Final.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: aaro.mil">[AARO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">aaro.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Go Fast Case Resolution</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">AARO GoFast Case ResolutionFebruary 24, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: February 24, 2025</span></span></span>

The underlying problem is called **parallax**: nearby objects appear to move across a background more quickly than distant objects when the observer changes position. A fighter aircraft travelling at high speed provides a constantly changing viewpoint, meaning the camera operator’s motion can become part of the apparent motion of the target.

This does not mean every unusual-looking FLIR movement is explained by perspective. It means that movement claims require measurements beyond the video frame. Analysts need aircraft position, heading, altitude, sensor line-of-sight data, range information, and ideally independent tracking from other systems.

The Gimbal video shows another interpretation challenge. The object appears to rotate, leading some viewers to suggest that it is performing an unusual manoeuvre. Other analyses have argued that the apparent rotation may involve infrared glare, optics, and the behaviour of a stabilised targeting system rather than the physical rotation of the object itself. The debate remains a useful example of how a compelling visual effect can arise from the interaction between a target and a complex sensor.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.wired.com/story/does-it-matter-that-the-dod-released-those-ufo-videos" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wired.com">[WIRED]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wired.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Does It Matter That the DOD Released Those UFO Videos?</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">These videos, previously surfaced through The New York Times in 2017, were not initially authorized by the Pentagon, though they were ack...</span></span></span>

The key lesson is that an unusual appearance is not the same as an unusual capability. A video can faithfully record what a sensor displayed while still leaving multiple explanations open.

## What missing measurements leave unresolved

The strongest limitation of FLIR UAP evidence is not that infrared systems are unreliable. Military infrared sensors are valuable and widely used. The limitation is that short clips often contain too little information to reconstruct the full event.

A scientifically useful observation would ideally include:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--metric" markdown="1">

* calibrated sensor data rather than only a compressed video;
* exact aircraft position and movement;

Without these inputs, investigators may be able to determine that an object was present while remaining unable to identify its nature or performance.

The 2004 USS Nimitz “FLIR1” footage illustrates the same issue. The video became central to discussions of a reported encounter involving Navy aircraft, but the publicly available clip is only one part of a much larger event. Questions about the object’s characteristics depend heavily on information outside the video itself, including radar records, pilot observations, and classified sensor data that are not fully available for public examination.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.wired.com/story/does-it-matter-that-the-dod-released-those-ufo-videos" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: wired.com">[WIRED]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">wired.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Does It Matter That the DOD Released Those UFO Videos?</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">These videos, previously surfaced through The New York Times in 2017, were not initially authorized by the Pentagon, though they were ack...</span></span></span>

## Why infrared footage remains valuable but limited

FLIR videos remain important because they are authentic records from sophisticated military systems. They can reveal events worthy of investigation and can sometimes help identify ordinary explanations. However, they should be treated as evidence requiring interpretation, not as self-explanatory recordings.

The central question is not whether a FLIR video looks strange. Many genuine objects can look strange when viewed through an infrared targeting system from a moving aircraft. The more useful question is whether the available measurements are sufficient to distinguish between competing explanations.

For UAP investigations, the difference between “unidentified” and “extraordinary” is largely a measurement problem. A short infrared clip may show something that was not immediately identified, but without range, calibration, and context, it rarely provides enough information to determine what physical process produced the image.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://smd-cms.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2023/09/uap-independent-study-team-final-report.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: smd-cms.nasa.gov">[NASA Science]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">smd-cms.nasa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Science NASA</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UNIDENTIFIED ANOMALOUS PHENOMENA Independent...</span></span></span>
