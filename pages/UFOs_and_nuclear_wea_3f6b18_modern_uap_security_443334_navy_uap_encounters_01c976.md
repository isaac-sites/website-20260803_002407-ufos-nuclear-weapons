## Introduction

Navy UAP encounters remain debated because they combine two things that rarely appear together: highly trained military observers and incomplete evidence. Cases such as the 2004 USS *Nimitz* “Tic Tac” encounter and the later *Gimbal* and *GoFast* videos involved fighter pilots, naval sensors, and official investigations, yet they have not produced a simple answer about what was observed. The central lesson is not that military sensors always reveal extraordinary technology, but that even advanced systems can produce ambiguous data when distance, tracking geometry, environmental conditions, and missing information limit interpretation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://uap-archive.org/uap/learn/pentagon-navy-uap-videos/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: uap-archive.org">[UAP Records Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">uap-archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on uap-archive.org.</span></span></span>

## The Nimitz encounter and later reports

### The 2004 “Tic Tac” case

The USS *Nimitz* encounter is the most influential Navy UAP case because it brought together multiple forms of observation: ship-based [radar reports]({{ 'radar-reports/' | relative_url }}), pilot testimony, and an infrared recording from an F/A-18 aircraft. During a training exercise off the coast of California in November 2004, Navy aviators reported being directed toward unusual radar contacts tracked by the USS *Princeton*. Two F/A-18 pilots, Commander David Fravor and Lieutenant Commander Alex Dietrich, described seeing a white, smooth, oblong object over the ocean. A separate F/A-18 crew later recorded infrared footage known as FLIR1.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://disclosurearchives.com/navy-uap-encounters" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: disclosurearchives.com">[Disclosure Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">disclosurearchives.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on disclosurearchives.com.</span></span></span>

The case became controversial because some interpretations suggested the object demonstrated extraordinary acceleration or manoeuvring. Supporters of the “anomalous” interpretation point to the combination of radar reports, experienced pilots, and the lack of an immediately identified conventional aircraft. Critics argue that the publicly available evidence is incomplete: key raw radar data, full sensor records, and detailed operational information have not been released, making precise reconstruction difficult.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://disclosurearchives.com/navy-uap-encounters" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: disclosurearchives.com">[Disclosure Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">disclosurearchives.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on disclosurearchives.com.</span></span></span>

The infrared video itself illustrates the problem. A sensor recording is not the same as a direct measurement of an object’s full behaviour. An infrared targeting pod shows heat contrast, tracking information, and camera geometry, but interpretation depends on factors such as range, angle, atmospheric conditions, and what the sensor was actually locked onto. The video confirms that a Navy aircraft recorded an unidentified object, but it does not independently establish every claimed performance characteristic.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://uap-archive.org/uap/learn/pentagon-navy-uap-videos/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: uap-archive.org">[UAP Records Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">uap-archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on uap-archive.org.</span></span></span>

### The 2015 Navy videos: Gimbal and GoFast

The debate expanded after the release of three Navy videos: FLIR1 from the 2004 Nimitz encounter, and the 2015 *Gimbal* and *GoFast* clips recorded during operations involving the USS *Theodore Roosevelt* carrier strike group. The Department of Defense officially released the videos in 2020 and confirmed that the footage was genuine Navy material, while noting that the phenomena shown remained classified as unidentified.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://uap-archive.org/uap/learn/pentagon-navy-uap-videos/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: uap-archive.org">[UAP Records Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">uap-archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on uap-archive.org.</span></span></span>

The *Gimbal* video attracted attention because the object appears to rotate while moving through the sky. Some analysts have argued that the apparent rotation may involve infrared glare, camera movement, or characteristics of the sensor system rather than the object itself physically rotating. Other analyses have continued to debate the geometry of the event and the available flight data.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://declassifiedtoday.com/2026/06/20/gimbal-and-gofast-the-navy-videos-the-pentagon-made-official/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: declassifiedtoday.com">[Declassified Today]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">declassifiedtoday.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Declassified TodayGimbal and GoFast: The Navy Videos the Pentagon Made OfficialJune 20, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 20, 2026</span></span></span>

The *GoFast* video became a particularly clear example of why sensor interpretation matters. The clip appears to show a small object moving rapidly above the ocean, but later analysis by the All-domain Anomaly Resolution Office (AARO) concluded that the apparent speed was strongly affected by viewing geometry and parallax — the apparent movement of an object caused by the observer’s motion and perspective. The object’s exact identity remained unresolved, but the analysis showed that an impressive-looking video can still have a conventional explanation for part of its appearance.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://uap-archive.org/uap/learn/pentagon-navy-uap-videos/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: uap-archive.org">[UAP Records Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">uap-archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on uap-archive.org.</span></span></span>

## Sensors, perception, and data limits

### Why advanced equipment can still create uncertainty

Military sensors are designed for detection, tracking, and decision-making, not always for public scientific identification of unknown objects. A radar system may detect a return without providing a complete picture of shape or intent. An infrared camera may capture a heat signature without revealing the object’s material, distance, or true movement. A pilot may make a rapid visual judgement while operating in demanding conditions at high speed.

This does not mean military observations are unreliable. Rather, reliability depends on the question being asked. A pilot’s statement that they saw something unusual may be strong evidence that an unusual observation occurred. It is a different question from whether the object’s speed, acceleration, or propulsion can be measured accurately from available data.

The UAP investigation challenge is therefore often a problem of incomplete measurement. A case may remain unresolved because investigators lack enough information to distinguish between several possibilities: a conventional object viewed under unusual conditions, an adversary system, a sensor effect, an environmental phenomenon, or a genuinely unexplained event. AARO’s public approach emphasises collecting better-quality data because uncertainty is frequently caused by missing or insufficient evidence rather than by evidence of extraordinary performance.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.aaro.mil/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: aaro.mil">[AARO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">aaro.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">AARO Home...</span></span></span>

### Human perception and machine data do not always align

Navy UAP cases often involve a combination of human and machine observation. This combination can strengthen a report, but it can also introduce complexity.

A pilot sees an object from one angle, with limited time to judge distance and speed. A sensor records information through a specific lens, tracking system, and processing chain. Radar operators may see symbols representing returns rather than a detailed physical object. Each source captures only part of the event.

The strongest investigations therefore attempt to combine independent data streams: radar records, infrared footage, flight telemetry, weather information, communications logs, and intelligence assessments. When these records are missing or incomplete, even experienced investigators may be unable to determine what happened with confidence.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.aaro.mil/Next-AARO-Home-redesign/Next-Parent/Next-UAP-Case-RR-Data-Table/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: aaro.mil">[AARO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">aaro.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-title">UAP Case RR Data Table</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">AARO UAP Case RR Data Table...</span></span></span>

## Separating unusual appearance from capability

One of the most persistent disagreements in Navy UAP discussions is whether an unusual appearance demonstrates unusual capability. The two are not the same.

A video showing an object that appears to move extremely fast does not automatically prove extreme velocity. A sensor image showing an object apparently rotating does not necessarily prove controlled manoeuvring. A radar contact does not by itself reveal whether the source is an aircraft, balloon, atmospheric effect, or another type of object.

The *GoFast* case demonstrates this distinction clearly: the visual impression suggested extraordinary speed, but geometric analysis indicated that the apparent movement was partly a perspective effect.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://uap-archive.org/uap/learn/pentagon-navy-uap-videos/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: uap-archive.org">[UAP Records Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">uap-archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on uap-archive.org.</span></span></span> Similar debates surround other Navy footage, where analysts disagree about whether unusual features reflect the object itself or limitations in interpreting sensor output.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://declassifiedtoday.com/2026/06/20/gimbal-and-gofast-the-navy-videos-the-pentagon-made-official/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: declassifiedtoday.com">[Declassified Today]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">declassifiedtoday.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Declassified TodayGimbal and GoFast: The Navy Videos the Pentagon Made OfficialJune 20, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 20, 2026</span></span></span>

For national security, the important issue is not whether every unexplained Navy encounter represents breakthrough technology. The operational concern is that [unidentified objects]({{ 'unidentified-objects/' | relative_url }}) can create uncertainty around military activity.
