## Introduction

Military radar anomalies made Cold War [UFO investigations]({{ 'ufo-case-investigation/' | relative_url }}) more complicated because radar appeared to offer something witnesses alone could not: an electronic record of objects in the sky. Yet radar was not a perfect “camera”. Its signals could be distorted by atmospheric conditions, reflections, equipment limitations, and operator interpretation. This meant that radar evidence could strengthen a UFO report while still leaving open major questions about what had actually been detected.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://files.ncas.org/condon/text/s6chap05.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: files.ncas.org">[NCAS Files]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">files.ncas.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Files Condon Report, Sec VI, Chapter 5 -- Radar and UFOs</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NCAS FilesCondon Report, Sec VI, Chapter 5 -- Radar and UFOs...</span></span></span>

During the Cold War, this uncertainty mattered most around military installations, air defence networks, and nuclear-related sites, where unknown objects were treated as possible security concerns. The challenge was not simply whether radar detected something unusual, but whether the signal represented a physical object, a known aircraft, or a misleading radar effect.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/research/military/air-force/ufos" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives Project BLUE BOOK</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesProject BLUE BOOK - Unidentified Flying Objects &#124; National ArchivesJune 25, 2024...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 25, 2024</span></span></span>

## How Cold War radar worked

Radar systems of the 1940s, 1950s, and 1960s were designed primarily for military surveillance: locating aircraft, guiding interceptors, and monitoring airspace. A radar set transmitted radio waves and analysed returning echoes to estimate an object’s position and movement. For defence operators, this capability was revolutionary because it allowed aircraft to be tracked beyond visual range and during poor visibility.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://files.ncas.org/condon/text/s6chap05.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: files.ncas.org">[NCAS Files]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">files.ncas.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Files Condon Report, Sec VI, Chapter 5 -- Radar and UFOs</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NCAS FilesCondon Report, Sec VI, Chapter 5 -- Radar and UFOs...</span></span></span>

However, a radar return was not automatically proof of a solid aircraft. The system detected reflected energy, and that energy could come from many sources. The scientific analysis later included in the University of Colorado’s [Condon Report]({{ 'condon-report/' | relative_url }}) noted that radar echoes could be produced by genuine targets as well as atmospheric conditions, reflections, system noise, and other effects that created difficult-to-interpret signals.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://files.ncas.org/condon/text/s6chap05.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: files.ncas.org">[NCAS Files]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">files.ncas.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Files Condon Report, Sec VI, Chapter 5 -- Radar and UFOs</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NCAS FilesCondon Report, Sec VI, Chapter 5 -- Radar and UFOs...</span></span></span>

This limitation was especially important during the Cold War because military radar operators were often looking for rare but serious threats, including unknown aircraft or missile activity. A strange return near a sensitive location could not simply be ignored. At the same time, the secrecy surrounding military technology meant that investigators and the public often lacked information needed to identify ordinary explanations.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/research/military/air-force/ufos" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives Project BLUE BOOK</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesProject BLUE BOOK - Unidentified Flying Objects &#124; National ArchivesJune 25, 2024...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 25, 2024</span></span></span>

### Why radar could create misleading targets

Radar depended heavily on the environment through which signals travelled. Temperature changes, humidity layers, and atmospheric boundaries could alter the path of radar energy, creating false or displaced returns. The Condon Report’s radar analysis described how atmospheric conditions could produce anomalous propagation, where radar signals travelled in unusual ways and generated apparent targets.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://files.ncas.org/condon/text/s3chap05.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: files.ncas.org">[NCAS Files]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">files.ncas.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Files Condon Report, Sec III, Chapter 5: Optical &amp; Radar Analysis</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NCAS FilesCondon Report, Sec III, Chapter 5: Optical &amp; Radar Analysis...</span></span></span>

Other problems came from reflections. Nearby terrain, aircraft structures, weather phenomena, and other objects could redirect radar energy and create confusing displays. The radar screen itself also required human interpretation: operators had to judge whether a signal represented a real object, background interference, or a technical artefact.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://files.ncas.org/condon/text/sec-ii.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: files.ncas.org">[NCAS Files]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">files.ncas.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Files Condon Report, Section II</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NCAS FilesCondon Report, Section II: Summary of the Study...</span></span></span>

These factors did not make radar useless. Military radar was a highly valuable detection tool. The difficulty was that unusual radar returns required careful analysis rather than immediate acceptance as evidence of an unknown craft.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://files.ncas.org/condon/text/s6chap05.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: files.ncas.org">[NCAS Files]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">files.ncas.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Files Condon Report, Sec VI, Chapter 5 -- Radar and UFOs</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NCAS FilesCondon Report, Sec VI, Chapter 5 -- Radar and UFOs...</span></span></span>

## Signal confusion and investigations

The most famous example of radar-related UFO uncertainty was the Washington, D.C. radar incident of July 1952. During two weekends that month, radar operators reported unidentified targets over the capital area, while some observers also reported unusual lights. Because the events occurred near important government facilities, they attracted significant military attention.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://files.ncas.org/condon/text/s3chap05.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: files.ncas.org">[NCAS Files]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">files.ncas.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Files Condon Report, Sec III, Chapter 5: Optical &amp; Radar Analysis</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NCAS FilesCondon Report, Sec III, Chapter 5: Optical &amp; Radar Analysis...</span></span></span>

The later Condon Report examined the radar evidence and concluded that the weather conditions at the time were capable of causing anomalous radar propagation. It identified temperature inversions and atmospheric conditions that could produce radar effects consistent with the reported unidentified returns. The report also concluded that many of the visual observations were likely explainable through astronomical or atmospheric causes, although the case remained historically important because it demonstrated how difficult radar-visual incidents could be to interpret.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://files.ncas.org/condon/text/s3chap05.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: files.ncas.org">[NCAS Files]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">files.ncas.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Files Condon Report, Sec III, Chapter 5: Optical &amp; Radar Analysis</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NCAS FilesCondon Report, Sec III, Chapter 5: Optical &amp; Radar Analysis...</span></span></span>

Another important category involved cases where radar and visual observations appeared to support each other. The 1956 Greenwich radar case investigated by the Condon team involved reported radar tracking, visual observations, and an attempted interception by aircraft. The case illustrates why radar evidence became influential: when multiple systems appeared to detect the same event, investigators had to consider explanations beyond a simple witness error. At the same time, the interpretation of radar data remained dependent on the quality of records and reconstruction available years later.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://files.ncas.org/condon/text/case02.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: files.ncas.org">[NCAS Files]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">files.ncas.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Files Condon Report, Case 2: USAF/RAF Radar Sighting</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NCAS FilesCondon Report, Case 2: USAF/RAF Radar Sighting...</span></span></span>

A similar pattern appeared in many [Project Blue Book]({{ 'project-blue-book/' | relative_url }}) investigations. The US Air Force programme collected thousands of reports between 1947 and 1969, including cases involving radar information. Official records preserved at the National Archives show that investigators considered radar, photographs, [witness testimony]({{ 'witness-disputes/' | relative_url }}), and other evidence together rather than treating any single source as decisive.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/research/military/air-force/ufos" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives Project BLUE BOOK</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesProject BLUE BOOK - Unidentified Flying Objects &#124; National ArchivesJune 25, 2024...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 25, 2024</span></span></span>

## Evaluating radar evidence

Radar reports became some of the strongest-looking UFO evidence of the Cold War because they seemed less vulnerable than eyewitness descriptions. A trained operator watching a radar display appeared to provide an objective measurement. The problem was that radar data still required interpretation. A signal on a screen showed that radio energy had returned, but not necessarily what caused that return.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://files.ncas.org/condon/text/sec-ii.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: files.ncas.org">[NCAS Files]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">files.ncas.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Files Condon Report, Section II</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NCAS FilesCondon Report, Section II: Summary of the Study...</span></span></span>

A careful evaluation of radar-based UFO cases usually depends on several questions:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--metric" markdown="1">

* **Was the radar system functioning correctly?** Equipment faults, calibration issues, or unusual operating conditions could affect results.
* **Was there independent confirmation?** A radar return supported by visual observation, another radar system, or recorded data carried more weight than a single unexplained signal.
* **Could the environment explain the return?** Weather, atmospheric layers, and reflections were important possibilities.
* **Was enough information available?** Many Cold War cases were investigated after the event, when radar recordings, logs, or classified information were incomplete.

</div>

The debate around radar anomalies shows why UFO investigations were difficult during the nuclear age. Military personnel were often dealing with genuine uncertainty: an unexplained radar return near a defence facility was worth investigating, but the technology itself could also generate confusing evidence. Radar therefore became both a tool for discovering unusual events and a source of new ambiguity.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://files.ncas.org/condon/text/s6chap05.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: files.ncas.org">[NCAS Files]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">files.ncas.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Files Condon Report, Sec VI, Chapter 5 -- Radar and UFOs</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NCAS FilesCondon Report, Sec VI, Chapter 5 -- Radar and UFOs...</span></span></span>

The lasting importance of Cold War radar cases is not that they proved every unexplained return represented an extraordinary object. Instead, they revealed a more complicated reality: advanced military sensors could detect events that were real but misunderstood, while the limits of those same sensors made interpretation difficult. In an era shaped by nuclear tension and technological secrecy, radar uncertainty became one of the main reasons UFO investigations remained unresolved.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/research/military/air-force/ufos" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives Project BLUE BOOK</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesProject BLUE BOOK - Unidentified Flying Objects &#124; National ArchivesJune 25, 2024...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 25, 2024</span></span></span>
