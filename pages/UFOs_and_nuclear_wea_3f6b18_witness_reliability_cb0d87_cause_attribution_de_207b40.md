## Introduction

Unidentified events connected to military and nuclear settings create a difficult evidence problem: a witness can be completely accurate about what they observed while still being unable to identify what caused it. This distinction sits at the centre of the debate over [UFOs and nuclear weapons]({{ 'ufos-and-nuclear-weapons/' | relative_url }}). A report of an unusual light, an unexpected object, or a technical disturbance may establish that something unusual was experienced, but it does not automatically establish that the event was caused by an unknown technology, a hostile actor, or an extraordinary source. Modern investigations by organisations such as the US Department of Defense’s All-domain Anomaly Resolution Office (AARO) and NASA have repeatedly emphasised that identifying an observation requires stronger data than testimony alone.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://uap-archive.org/uap/learn/aaro-historical-record-report-findings/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: uap-archive.org">[UAP Records Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">uap-archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">aaro historical record report findings</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UAP Records ArchiveWhat the AARO Historical Record Report Found (2024) &#124; UAP Records Archive...</span></span></span>

The reliability debate therefore turns on a narrow but important question: when military personnel describe something strange, how far can investigators reasonably go from “this happened” to “this happened because of a specific cause”? The strongest evidence-based approach separates the observation itself from later explanations, treating cause attribution as a separate investigative step requiring independent confirmation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://uap-archive.org/uap/records/aaro-historical-record-report-vol1/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: uap-archive.org">[UAP Records Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">uap-archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UAP Records ArchiveAARO Report on the Historical Record of U.S. Government Involvement with UAP, Volume I &#124; UAP Records ArchiveFebruary 1...</span></span></span>

## Separating observation from interpretation

Military witnesses often provide valuable information because they may have training, access to technical systems, and experience with unusual environments. A missile officer, radar operator, or pilot may be well positioned to recognise that an event did not match normal expectations. However, expertise usually improves observation; it does not make interpretation infallible.

A useful distinction is:

* **Observation reliability:** Did the person accurately report what they saw, heard, measured, or experienced?
* **Attribution reliability:** Is the proposed explanation for that event supported by enough evidence?

These two questions are frequently combined in public discussions, but investigators treat them differently. A witness may reliably report that a missile system malfunctioned shortly after an unusual sighting. That does not, by itself, prove that the sighting caused the malfunction. Establishing causation requires evidence linking the two events through timing, mechanism, and independent measurements.

This issue appears repeatedly in historical UAP investigations. [Project Blue Book]({{ 'project-blue-book/' | relative_url }}), the US Air Force investigation programme that examined thousands of reports between 1947 and 1969, preserved many cases where witnesses genuinely reported unusual events. Yet the existence of unidentified reports did not demonstrate an [extraterrestrial]({{ 'extraterrestrial/' | relative_url }}) or otherwise extraordinary cause. The National Archives records that Blue Book reviewed 12,618 reports, with 701 remaining unidentified at the programme’s conclusion, while the Air Force found no evidence that unidentified cases represented extraterrestrial vehicles or technologies beyond known scientific understanding.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.archives.gov/research/military/air-force/ufos" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: archives.gov">[National Archives]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">archives.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Archives Project BLUE BOOK</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National ArchivesProject BLUE BOOK - Unidentified Flying Objects &#124; National ArchivesJune 25, 2024...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 25, 2024</span></span></span>

The same problem applies to modern military encounters. [Sensor data]({{ 'sensor-data/' | relative_url }}), eyewitness accounts, and recordings can show that an event occurred, but they may not contain enough information to determine distance, speed, object type, or origin. NASA’s UAP study noted that many observations contain limited data, making scientific conclusions about their nature difficult.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://science.nasa.gov/uap/faqs/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: science.nasa.gov">[NASA Science]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">science.nasa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Science UAP FAQs</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NASA ScienceUAP FAQs - NASA Science...</span></span></span>

## The nuclear weapons cases and the cause question

Nuclear-related UFO claims are among the most debated because the settings involved highly controlled military systems. Reports from missile facilities carry more weight for some researchers because the witnesses were not casual observers; they were personnel responsible for maintaining and operating sensitive equipment.

The 1967 Malmstrom Air Force Base incidents in Montana illustrate the central disagreement. Former missile personnel have described episodes in which Minuteman missiles became temporarily unavailable while unusual objects were reportedly observed nearby. Supporters of the accounts argue that missile crews understood their systems and would have recognised abnormal events. The witnesses’ military roles are therefore presented as a reason to take the reports seriously.

The unresolved issue is not simply whether the personnel experienced something unusual. The harder question is whether the unusual object and the missile malfunction shared a cause. A coincidence in timing does not establish a mechanism. Investigators would need evidence showing how an external object affected the missile systems, supported by records, technical analysis, or multiple independent measurements.

This distinction explains why [official reviews]({{ 'official-review/' | relative_url }}) have been cautious. AARO’s historical review examined decades of US government investigations and reported no evidence that any US government investigation or official review had confirmed a UAP sighting as extraterrestrial technology. It also found no empirical evidence supporting claims of recovered non-human technology or reverse-engineered craft.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://uap-archive.org/uap/learn/aaro-historical-record-report-findings/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: uap-archive.org">[UAP Records Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">uap-archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">aaro historical record report findings</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UAP Records ArchiveWhat the AARO Historical Record Report Found (2024) &#124; UAP Records Archive...</span></span></span>

The conclusion does not mean every reported event has been fully explained. Rather, it separates two claims that are often merged: “military personnel reported unusual events” and “those events were caused by extraordinary technology.” The first has extensive documentation; the second requires a much higher evidential standard.

## Common errors in causal conclusions

### Mistaking sequence for causation

One of the most common reasoning errors is assuming that because one event happened before another, it caused it. In military settings this problem can be especially difficult because multiple systems operate simultaneously. A radar anomaly, communications problem, equipment failure, weather event, training activity, or classified test may occur in the same timeframe as an unusual observation.

A strong causal claim needs more than timing. Investigators typically look for:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* a plausible physical mechanism;
* reliable measurements showing the relationship;
* independent confirmation from separate sources;
* evidence ruling out more ordinary explanations.

</div>

Without these elements, an unusual event remains a genuine observation but not a confirmed explanation.

### Treating “unidentified” as a final answer

Another frequent mistake is assuming that an unidentified event is automatically evidence for the most unusual explanation. In investigative terminology, “unidentified” usually means that available information is insufficient to determine the cause.

This distinction has been central to recent UAP reporting. AARO has stated that many reported cases are resolved through ordinary explanations such as balloons, drones, aircraft, satellites, or sensor issues, while some remain unresolved because the available data is insufficient.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://apnews.com/article/5638be273b753253713a478546849e46" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: apnews.com">[AP News]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">apnews.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The report includes many misidentified objects such as balloons, birds, and satellites, though some cases remain unexplained due to insuf...</span></span></span>

An unresolved case can therefore have several possible explanations:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--caution" markdown="1">

* an ordinary object that was poorly observed;
* a known technology with incomplete information;
* a sensor or recording limitation;
* an unusual natural phenomenon;
* a genuinely unknown mechanism.

</div>

The category describes the state of knowledge, not the identity of the cause.

### Overestimating the power of expert testimony

Expert witnesses can provide important context, but expertise has limits. A fighter pilot may accurately describe an object’s movement but misjudge its distance. A technician may correctly report a system failure but incorrectly attribute its source. Human perception and memory remain affected by factors such as viewing conditions, stress, limited reference points, and the passage of time.

The 1968 [Condon Report]({{ 'condon-report/' | relative_url }})’s discussion of UFO reports examined how perception and cognition influence witness descriptions, noting that a person’s interpretation of what they perceived can become part of the report itself.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://files.ncas.org/condon/text/s6chap01.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: files.ncas.org">[NCAS Files]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">files.ncas.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Files Condon Report, Sec VI, Chapter 1 -- Perceptual Problems</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NCAS FilesCondon Report, Sec VI, Chapter 1 -- Perceptual Problems...</span></span></span>

This does not mean witnesses are unreliable. It means investigators must distinguish between a reliable account of an experience and a reliable explanation of that experience.

## How investigators weigh competing explanations

A disciplined investigation begins with the least assumption-heavy question: what is the evidence directly showing? Investigators then compare explanations according to how well they account for all available information.

For a nuclear-related unidentified event, a typical assessment might examine:

1. **What was directly observed?**

Witness statements, radar returns, photographs, communications records, maintenance logs, and environmental information are separated from later interpretations.
2. **What explanations fit the evidence?**

Investigators compare possibilities including equipment problems, atmospheric effects, human error, conventional aircraft, drones, classified activities, and other causes.
3. **What evidence would discriminate between explanations?**

A stronger conclusion requires information that one explanation predicts and alternatives do not.

This approach is why recent scientific and government reviews have focused heavily on improving data collection rather than relying only on [witness testimony]({{ 'witness-disputes/' | relative_url }}). NASA recommended better-quality observations and more systematic data gathering because limited information prevents reliable conclusions about many UAP reports.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://science.nasa.gov/uap/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: science.nasa.gov">[NASA Science]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">science.nasa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Science UAP</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NASA ScienceUAP - NASA Science...</span></span></span>

The broader lesson for the UFO and nuclear weapons debate is that witnesses can be central to establishing that an event deserves investigation. They may identify genuine anomalies that would otherwise be ignored. But the transition from “something unusual happened” to “we know what caused it” requires a different type of evidence.

Cause attribution is therefore the dividing line between documenting an unexplained event and proving an explanation. In military nuclear cases especially, the most careful assessments recognise both sides of the debate: trained witnesses can provide significant evidence of unusual experiences, while extraordinary causal claims still require extraordinary confirmation.
