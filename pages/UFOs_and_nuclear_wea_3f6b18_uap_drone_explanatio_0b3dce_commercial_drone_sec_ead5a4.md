## Introduction

Commercial drones have changed the security picture around nuclear facilities because they create a new category of aerial activity that can be both ordinary and concerning at the same time. A small consumer drone near a nuclear plant may be a hobbyist aircraft, a journalist’s camera platform, a contractor’s tool, or an intentional surveillance device. The challenge is not only identifying what is flying, but determining who controls it, why it is there, and whether it represents a meaningful security risk. The US Nuclear Regulatory Commission (NRC) has recognised the growth of drone activity around critical infrastructure and requires nuclear power plant operators to report drone sightings to relevant authorities.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nrc.gov/reading-rm/doc-collections/fact-sheets/fs-drone-pwr-plant-security" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nrc.gov">[Nuclear Regulatory Commission]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nrc.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Nuclear Regulatory CommissionDrones and Nuclear Power Plant Security &#124; Nuclear Regulatory CommissionJanuary 21, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 21, 2025</span></span></span>

This distinction is important in discussions of UAP (unidentified anomalous phenomena) near nuclear sites. A drone explanation can resolve some unusual sightings because modern unmanned aircraft can look unfamiliar, move differently from conventional aircraft, and operate in places where observers do not expect them. But identifying an object as a drone does not automatically remove the security concern: an identified drone can still raise questions about access, surveillance, intent, and response capability.

## Commercial drone activity at sensitive sites

The expansion of consumer and commercial drone markets has made aerial access easier than in previous decades. A person no longer needs an aircraft licence or a large aviation platform to place a camera-equipped vehicle near a restricted area. Small drones can be purchased commercially, operated with limited training, and used for legitimate activities such as inspection, photography, mapping, and infrastructure surveys. The same accessibility creates a security challenge when drones appear near facilities where information gathering or unauthorised observation could be valuable.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-22-105705" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gao.gov">[Government Accountability Office]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gao.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">gao 22 105705</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Government Accountability OfficeScience &amp; Tech Spotlight: Counter-Drone Technologies &#124; U.S. GAOMarch 15, 2022...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 15, 2022</span></span></span>

Nuclear facilities are particularly sensitive because they combine public safety responsibilities with national security concerns. The issue is not that every drone near a plant represents a hostile act. Nuclear sites already use multiple layers of protection, including physical barriers, surveillance systems, and trained security personnel. The NRC states that nuclear power plants are designed with robust security measures and that operators coordinate with agencies when drone sightings occur.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nrc.gov/reading-rm/doc-collections/fact-sheets/fs-drone-pwr-plant-security" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nrc.gov">[Nuclear Regulatory Commission]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nrc.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Nuclear Regulatory CommissionDrones and Nuclear Power Plant Security &#124; Nuclear Regulatory CommissionJanuary 21, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 21, 2025</span></span></span>

The practical problem is that the same aircraft type can have very different meanings depending on context:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* A maintenance contractor’s drone may be authorised and documented.
* A hobby drone flown near a restricted boundary may be careless but not malicious.
* A drone repeatedly observing a facility may indicate surveillance activity.
* A drone carrying unknown equipment creates uncertainty even if it is technically identified.

</div>

This is why drone incidents around nuclear sites are not simply an aviation-identification problem. The security question begins after identification: determining ownership, purpose, and potential consequences.

## Why ordinary drones can appear unusual

The connection between drones and UAP discussions comes partly from the way small unmanned aircraft interact with human perception and existing surveillance systems. A drone seen at night may appear only as a moving light. From a distance, observers may not immediately recognise its size, speed, or flight limitations. Infrared cameras, radar systems, and human witnesses can each provide incomplete pictures of the same object.

Modern drones also have characteristics that can seem unusual compared with traditional aircraft. They can hover, remain stationary relative to the ground, move at low altitude, and operate without obvious aircraft features such as wings or engine noise. These traits can contribute to uncertainty when an observer lacks information about the platform involved.

However, a drone explanation should not be treated as a universal answer for every unexplained aerial event. The stronger analytical approach is to separate two questions:

1. **Can the object be explained as a known drone or another conventional aircraft?**
2. **If it is a drone, what does its presence mean from a security perspective?**

A confirmed drone may resolve the first question while leaving the second unanswered.

## Security concerns beyond object identification

The most significant risk from drones near nuclear facilities is not necessarily the aircraft itself, but the information and access it can provide. A drone does not need to cause physical damage to create a security concern. Repeated flights could reveal patterns of activity, security arrangements, facility layouts, or operational routines.

### Surveillance and intelligence gathering

Commercial drones can carry high-quality cameras and other sensors. The same capabilities that make them useful for industrial inspection also make them useful for unauthorised observation. Government security assessments have highlighted that drones can be used around sensitive locations for intelligence collection and other disruptive activities.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-22-105705" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gao.gov">[Government Accountability Office]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gao.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">gao 22 105705</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Government Accountability OfficeScience &amp; Tech Spotlight: Counter-Drone Technologies &#124; U.S. GAOMarch 15, 2022...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 15, 2022</span></span></span>

This creates a difficult distinction for security teams. A drone hovering near a facility may not provide enough evidence to determine whether it is:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* a curious operator testing boundaries,
* a photographer seeking unusual footage,
* an activist attempting documentation,
* a criminal gathering information,
* or a state-linked actor conducting reconnaissance.

</div>

The technology is relatively accessible, but attribution remains difficult.

### Physical and operational risks

Security concerns also include the possibility of drones carrying additional equipment or interfering with normal operations. Counter-drone research focuses on detection, tracking, identification, and mitigation because small unmanned aircraft can be difficult to monitor consistently. The US Government Accountability Office notes that counter-UAS systems face challenges including false detections, limited effectiveness, and potential unintended effects from mitigation measures.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-22-105705" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: gao.gov">[Government Accountability Office]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">gao.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">gao 22 105705</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Government Accountability OfficeScience &amp; Tech Spotlight: Counter-Drone Technologies &#124; U.S. GAOMarch 15, 2022...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 15, 2022</span></span></span>

For nuclear operators, this creates a balancing problem. A response must be strong enough to address a genuine threat while avoiding unsafe actions around complex industrial sites. A drone that appears suspicious still requires careful assessment before intervention.

## Tracking and attribution challenges

Finding a drone is only part of the problem. Determining who operates it and why can be much harder.

Small drones often present technical difficulties for detection systems because they fly at low altitude, have small radar signatures, and may operate among other airborne objects. Counter-drone systems commonly combine different approaches, including radar, radio-frequency detection, optical sensors, and acoustic monitoring, because no single method works reliably in every environment.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2008.12461" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: arxiv.org">[arXiv]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">arxiv.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Counter-Unmanned Aircraft System(s) (C-UAS): State of the Art, Challenges and Future TrendsAugust 28, 2020...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 28, 2020</span></span></span>

Attribution creates an additional layer of uncertainty. Even when a drone is detected, investigators may need to determine:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* where it was launched;
* who owns it;
* whether the operator was nearby or remote;
* whether the flight was accidental or deliberate;
* whether similar activity has occurred before.

</div>

Commercial drones complicate this because legitimate ownership is widespread. A device may be registered, modified, borrowed, or operated by someone other than its owner. Identifying the aircraft does not always identify the person responsible.

Regulatory measures attempt to improve accountability. The Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) has established restrictions on drone flights over certain sensitive locations, including nuclear facilities, and continues to develop policies aimed at managing risks around critical infrastructure.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.faa.gov/uas/critical_infrastructure" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: faa.gov">[Federal Aviation Administration]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">faa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Federal Aviation AdministrationCritical Infrastructure and Public Venues &#124; Federal Aviation AdministrationFebruary 22, 2023...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: February 22, 2023</span></span></span>

## The nuclear-security lesson from drone incidents

The growth of drone activity changes how unusual aerial observations near nuclear facilities should be interpreted. A report of a strange object no longer needs to begin with the assumption of an unknown aircraft category; commercially available drones are a realistic explanation for many observations. At the same time, a drone explanation does not mean the event is harmless.

The key security issue is uncertainty. Nuclear facilities must distinguish between ordinary drone activity and activity that signals surveillance, intrusion attempts, or preparation for a more serious action. This requires better detection, reliable reporting channels, and the ability to connect an aerial observation with a responsible operator.

In the broader [UAP-versus-drone]({{ 'uap-versus-drone/' | relative_url }}) discussion, drones provide an important middle ground. They explain why some sightings near sensitive sites may appear extraordinary while remaining conventional, but they also demonstrate why conventional technology can create genuine security challenges. The unresolved question is often not whether a drone can exist near a nuclear facility, but whether authorities can rapidly determine who is operating it, what information it is collecting, and whether its presence requires a security response.
