## Introduction

Reports of drones near US nuclear power plants have increased attention on how regulators detect, record, and respond to unauthorised activity around sensitive facilities. The rise does not show that every sighting represents a hostile operation, but it has created a clearer security issue: nuclear operators and government agencies must determine whether an object is a harmless aircraft, a rule violation, a surveillance attempt, or a more serious threat. The Nuclear Regulatory Commission (NRC) has acknowledged that the growing availability of commercial unmanned aircraft systems has led to numerous drone sightings over critical infrastructure, including nuclear plants, and it introduced reporting requirements to improve awareness of these events.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nrc.gov/reading-rm/doc-collections/fact-sheets/fs-drone-pwr-plant-security" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nrc.gov">[Nuclear Regulatory Commission]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nrc.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Nuclear Regulatory CommissionDrones and Nuclear Power Plant Security &#124; Nuclear Regulatory CommissionJanuary 21, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 21, 2025</span></span></span>

The issue sits within the wider discussion of unidentified activity around nuclear sites because some reports initially involve unknown objects. However, the strongest documented evidence concerns conventional drones and the challenges of attribution, regulation, and response rather than proof of unexplained technology. The central governance question is how US authorities can maintain security around nuclear facilities while distinguishing genuine risks from the large number of ordinary drone activities now occurring across the country.

## Rise in reported drone activity

### A small dataset revealed a larger security concern

One of the clearest indicators of increased attention came in late 2024, when data provided by the NRC showed a sharp rise in reported drone events at nuclear facilities during a period of heightened public concern about drone sightings across the United States. Reports collected by *The War Zone* indicated that nuclear facility licensees had reported 15 drone events from the start of the year through 10 December 2024, with the total increasing to 26 by 17 December. The increase occurred alongside a broader wave of drone reports near military installations and infrastructure, although officials did not establish a direct connection between the incidents.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://endtimeheadlines.org/2024/12/nuclear-power-plants-are-reporting-a-massive-uptick-in-drone-sightings/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: endtimeheadlines.org">[End Time Headlines]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">endtimeheadlines.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">End Time HeadlinesNuclear power plants are reporting a massive uptick in drone sightingsDecember 22, 2024...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 22, 2024</span></span></span>

The figures are significant because they show how reporting systems can reveal patterns that may not be visible from individual incidents. A single drone near a restricted area may be accidental, but repeated reports across multiple sites raise questions about whether operators are seeing more drone activity, becoming better at identifying it, or responding more consistently after public attention increases.

The available numbers should also be interpreted carefully. A higher number of reports does not automatically mean a higher number of deliberate attacks. Increased public awareness, improved reporting channels, and regulatory changes can all influence the dataset. The value of the data is therefore less about proving intent and more about showing where security systems are encountering uncertainty.

### Why nuclear plants treat sightings differently

Nuclear facilities operate under strict security requirements because even non-destructive activity can create operational concerns. A drone may provide an external observer with images of a site layout, security arrangements, or response procedures. It may also force security personnel to investigate an unknown object, creating costs and requiring coordination with other agencies.

The NRC’s updated guidance reflects this shift. The agency states that nuclear plant licensees are required to report drone sightings over their facilities, with information shared with agencies including the Federal Aviation Administration (FAA), Federal Bureau of Investigation (FBI), and local law enforcement. The NRC also notes that commercial nuclear plants already maintain security measures such as physical barriers, surveillance systems, and trained security forces, but drone incidents add a newer type of airborne challenge.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nrc.gov/reading-rm/doc-collections/fact-sheets/fs-drone-pwr-plant-security" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nrc.gov">[Nuclear Regulatory Commission]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nrc.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Nuclear Regulatory CommissionDrones and Nuclear Power Plant Security &#124; Nuclear Regulatory CommissionJanuary 21, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 21, 2025</span></span></span>

## Regulatory attention and restrictions

### Reporting became part of the response system

The increase in drone reports has pushed regulators towards better information collection rather than treating every event as an isolated incident. The NRC’s approach is focused on understanding trends, assessing whether security assumptions remain adequate, and coordinating with agencies responsible for airspace management and law enforcement.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nrc.gov/reading-rm/doc-collections/fact-sheets/fs-drone-pwr-plant-security" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nrc.gov">[Nuclear Regulatory Commission]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nrc.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Nuclear Regulatory CommissionDrones and Nuclear Power Plant Security &#124; Nuclear Regulatory CommissionJanuary 21, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 21, 2025</span></span></span>

This approach reflects a broader challenge in critical infrastructure security: detection is often easier than attribution. A security team may be able to determine that a drone entered restricted airspace, but identifying the operator, purpose, or level of risk can be much harder.

Research on drone detection highlights why this problem is technically difficult. Small unmanned aircraft can be detected using combinations of radar, optical systems, acoustic sensors, and radio-frequency monitoring, but each method has limitations depending on weather, terrain, distance, and the type of drone involved. Studies of protected-airspace detection systems have found that combining multiple sensing methods is generally more reliable than relying on a single technology.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2111.03760" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: arxiv.org">[arXiv]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">arxiv.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">arXiv Securing your Airspace: Detection of Drones Trespassing Protected Areas</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Securing your Airspace: Detection of Drones Trespassing Protected AreasNovember 5, 2021...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 5, 2021</span></span></span>

### Restrictions have expanded around sensitive sites

The US response has also included tighter controls on where drones may operate. The nuclear industry began coordinating with federal agencies to restrict drone overflights around certain nuclear power plants, while other sensitive locations have used temporary flight restrictions and enforcement measures to reduce unauthorised activity.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nrc.gov/reading-rm/doc-collections/fact-sheets/fs-drone-pwr-plant-security" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nrc.gov">[Nuclear Regulatory Commission]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nrc.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Nuclear Regulatory CommissionDrones and Nuclear Power Plant Security &#124; Nuclear Regulatory CommissionJanuary 21, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 21, 2025</span></span></span>

However, regulation faces practical limits. Nuclear operators generally cannot simply disable or destroy an unknown drone flying nearby. Counter-drone actions involve legal authorities, aviation rules, and coordination between agencies. This creates a gap between detecting a potential problem and taking immediate action against it.

## Questions about intent and attribution

### More reports do not automatically reveal the motive

The most difficult question surrounding increased drone reports is why the drones are appearing. Several explanations remain possible: hobbyists entering restricted areas, commercial operators making mistakes, journalists or observers seeking footage, security testing, criminal activity, or deliberate intelligence collection.

Government agencies have generally avoided assigning a hostile motive to unexplained drone sightings without evidence. This caution is important because nuclear facilities operate in a security environment where false alarms can consume resources while premature accusations can create unnecessary escalation.

The same uncertainty has shaped public debate around drones and unidentified aerial activity near sensitive sites. Because nuclear facilities have historically been linked with UFO discussions, new drone reports can attract speculation beyond the available evidence. The documented security issue, however, is more concrete: authorities are dealing with identifiable drone technology that can enter protected airspace and create uncertainty.

### The evidence gap remains the central issue

The current dataset of [nuclear plant drone]({{ 'plant-sightings/' | relative_url }}) reports provides evidence of increased reporting activity, but it does not by itself establish a coordinated campaign or a single cause. The strongest conclusion is that drone incursions have become a recognised governance issue for US [nuclear security]({{ 'security-concerns-0719dd/' | relative_url }}).

For regulators, the challenge is balancing preparedness with proportionality. Too little attention could leave facilities exposed to emerging risks, while treating every sighting as a major threat could overwhelm response systems and distort risk assessment.     closeMenu(section);
  }
  sections.forEach(function (section) {
    if (section.getAttribute('data-ebay-localized-init') === '1') return;
    section.setAttribute('data-ebay-localized-init', '1');
    var available = availableMarkets(section);
    var marketId = defaultMarket;
    if (window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation && window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation.inferMarket) {
      marketId = window.PhoenixAffiliateLocation.inferMarket(available, {
        storageKey: 'phoenix-ebay-market',
        defaultMarket: defaultMarket
      });
    } else if (available.indexOf(defaultMarket)< 0) {
      marketId = available[0] || defaultMarket;
    }
    var select = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-select]');
    if (select) {
      select.addEventListener('change', function () {
        applyMarket(section, select.value, true);
      });
    }
    var trigger = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-trigger]');
    var menu = section.querySelector('[data-ebay-market-menu]');
    if (trigger && menu) {
      trigger.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
        event.stopPropagation();
        var expanded = trigger.getAttribute('aria-expanded') === 'true';
        menu.hidden = expanded;
        trigger.setAttribute('aria-expanded', expanded ? 'false' : 'true');
        var picker = trigger.closest('.fr-ebay-market-picker');
        if (picker) picker.classList.toggle('fr-ebay-market-picker--open', !expanded);
      });
      Array.prototype.slice.call(menu.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-option]')).forEach(function (button) {
        button.addEventListener('click', function (event) {
          event.stopPropagation();
          applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-option'), true);
        });
      });
      document.addEventListener('click', function () { closeMenu(section); });
    }
    Array.prototype.slice.call(section.querySelectorAll('[data-ebay-market-button]')).forEach(function (button) {
      button.addEventListener('click', function () {
        applyMarket(section, button.getAttribute('data-ebay-market-button'), true);
      });
    });
    applyMarket(section, marketId, false);
  });
})();
