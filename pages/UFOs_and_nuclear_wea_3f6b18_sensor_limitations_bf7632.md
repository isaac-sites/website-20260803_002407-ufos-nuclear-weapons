## Introduction

This limitation is especially important in reports connected to nuclear weapons facilities, where historical incidents often rely on witness accounts, partial records, and sensors designed for security or military operations rather than scientific investigation. The result is a recurring evidence problem: an event may be considered important enough to investigate while still lacking the measurements needed to identify its cause.

## Why military sensors can leave UAP cases unresolved

Military systems are built for specific missions. A radar designed to detect aircraft, a missile-warning system designed to track launches, or an infrared targeting pod designed to assist pilots is not automatically a complete scientific observation platform. These systems may record an object’s presence without providing the information needed to determine its size, distance, speed, composition, or behaviour.

AARO has repeatedly identified insufficient data as a major barrier to resolving UAP reports. Its historical review stated that sensors and human observations are imperfect and that many cases lack “actionable data” or contain only limited-quality information. The office concluded that better-quality data would likely allow many unresolved cases to be explained.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://uap-archive.org/uap/records/aaro-historical-record-report-vol1/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: uap-archive.org">[UAP Records Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">uap-archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on uap-archive.org.</span></span></span>

Several recurring sensor problems affect UAP investigations:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--metric" markdown="1">

* **Range uncertainty:** A camera may show an object but cannot determine distance without additional measurements. A small nearby object and a large distant object can appear similar.
* **Limited field of view:** Aircraft-mounted cameras often record only a narrow portion of the sky, making it difficult to understand an object’s wider movement.
* **Missing metadata:** Investigators may lack exact information about sensor settings, location, altitude, environmental conditions, or tracking data.
* **Single-sensor dependence:** A video, radar return, or eyewitness account alone often cannot confirm whether an observation reflects a real object, a sensor effect, or a misinterpretation.

</div>

NASA’s independent UAP study reached a similar conclusion, noting that the limited number of high-quality observations makes firm scientific conclusions difficult and recommending better-calibrated, multi-sensor data collection.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nasa.gov/news-release/update-nasa-shares-uap-independent-study-report-names-director/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nasa.gov">[NASA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nasa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">UPDATE: NASA Shares UAP Independent Study Report; Names Director</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UPDATE: NASA Shares UAP Independent Study Report; Names Director - NASASeptember 14, 2023...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: September 14, 2023</span></span></span>

## The problem with infrared videos and apparent extreme movement

Many modern UAP discussions focus on military infrared videos because they appear to show unusual objects. However, infrared sensors measure heat signatures rather than providing a complete visual picture. Interpreting these recordings requires knowledge of the sensor’s optics, tracking mode, distance calculations, atmospheric conditions, and the motion of the observing aircraft.

The Navy videos commonly known as FLIR, Gimbal, and GoFast illustrate this challenge. They are genuine military recordings, but their interpretation depends heavily on understanding sensor behaviour. In the GoFast case, AARO’s analysis concluded that the apparent extreme speed was influenced by perspective effects and that the object was much farther away than it appeared from the footage alone.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.aaro.mil/UAP-Cases/Official-UAP-Imagery/4/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: aaro.mil">[AARO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">aaro.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-title">UAP Imagery</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">AARO UAP Imagery...</span></span></span>

The broader lesson is that a recording showing unusual appearance or movement does not automatically reveal unusual physical performance. Without independent measurements such as confirmed distance, acceleration data, multiple viewing angles, or correlated radar information, apparent manoeuvres can be misleading.

Infrared systems can also create ambiguous signatures. A thermal image may represent a hot object, reflected heat, atmospheric effects, or characteristics of the sensor itself. In one unresolved AARO case involving infrared footage from a US military platform, investigators noted that without corroborating telemetry or additional sensor data they could not determine whether the thermal signature came from a physical object or a sensor-related effect.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.aaro.mil/UAP-Cases/Official-UAP-Imagery/4/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: aaro.mil">[AARO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">aaro.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-title">UAP Imagery</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">AARO UAP Imagery...</span></span></span>

## Nuclear-related cases and the evidence gap

Reports linking UAP with nuclear weapons often involve some of the most sensitive military environments, including missile fields and weapons storage areas. These locations create a strong expectation that sophisticated monitoring systems should provide clear answers. However, historical cases frequently involve incomplete records rather than continuous, scientifically controlled observation.

The 1967 Malmstrom Air Force Base missile shutdown reports are among the best-known examples. Former personnel have described incidents in which unidentified lights were reported near missile facilities during periods when Minuteman missiles experienced failures. These accounts have become a major part of claims that UAP interacted with nuclear systems. However, public evidence has not established that an external object caused the missile problems, and the available record does not provide the type of multi-sensor data needed to demonstrate such a connection.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://nypost.com/2026/03/17/us-news/former-air-force-missile-officer-claims-ufos-disabled-nuclear-arsenal-at-montana-base-during-cold-war/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nypost.com">[New York Post]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nypost.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Air Force officer Robert Salas claimed that unidentified flying objects (UFOs) disabled 10 nuclear missiles at Malmstrom Air Force Base i...</span></span></span>

The sensor limitation in cases like Malmstrom is not necessarily that nothing was observed. Rather, the problem is that the observations do not provide a complete chain of evidence. A light seen by security personnel, a technical failure recorded by missile systems, and a possible relationship between the two are separate pieces of information. Establishing causation would require evidence showing how one event produced the other.

## Human observation remains an important but limited sensor

[Witness testimony]({{ 'witness-disputes/' | relative_url }}) has played a major role in nuclear-related UAP reports because military personnel are often highly trained observers. Pilots, security guards, and missile operators may notice unusual events that deserve investigation. However, human perception also has limitations, particularly during unusual or stressful situations.

At night, without reliable reference points, judging an object’s distance, speed, or size can be extremely difficult. A bright light may appear close while actually being far away, and unusual atmospheric conditions can alter how objects are perceived. These challenges apply even to experienced observers.

Official UAP investigations therefore treat eyewitness accounts as valuable evidence but usually seek confirmation through independent sources such as radar, satellite data, aircraft telemetry, or additional observers. AARO has noted that many reports remain unidentified simply because sensors did not collect enough information to make a positive identification.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.aaro.mil/FAQ/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: aaro.mil">[AARO]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">aaro.mil</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">AARO FAQ...</span></span></span>

## Why better sensors matter more than more sightings

The main challenge for UAP investigations is not a shortage of reports. Thousands of observations have been collected from military personnel, pilots, and civilians. The difficulty is producing observations detailed enough to support reliable conclusions.

Future investigations increasingly focus on combining multiple sensor types:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* optical cameras for visible characteristics;
* infrared systems for heat signatures;
* radar for distance and movement;
* radio-frequency sensors for emissions;
* environmental sensors for weather and atmospheric conditions.

</div>

Researchers studying scientific approaches to UAP investigation have argued that multi-modal systems reduce the risk of confusing sensor artefacts with genuine objects because independent measurements can be compared.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.18566" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: arxiv.org">[arXiv]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">arxiv.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Scientific Investigation of Unidentified Aerial Phenomena (UAP) Using Multimodal Ground-Based ObservatoriesMay 29, 2023...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 29, 2023</span></span></span> NASA has similarly highlighted the importance of systematic data collection, calibration, and sensor metadata to improve future analysis.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nasa.gov/news-release/update-nasa-shares-uap-independent-study-report-names-director/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nasa.gov">[NASA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nasa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">UPDATE: NASA Shares UAP Independent Study Report; Names Director</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UPDATE: NASA Shares UAP Independent Study Report; Names Director - NASASeptember 14, 2023...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: September 14, 2023</span></span></span>

For nuclear-related cases, this approach is particularly significant. A single unexplained observation near a missile facility may remain permanently ambiguous, but a coordinated record showing an object’s exact position, movement, electromagnetic characteristics, and interaction with systems would provide a much stronger basis for assessment.

## The unresolved does not automatically mean extraordinary

Sensor limitations create a difficult middle ground in UAP investigations. An unresolved case is not the same as proof of advanced technology, but it is also not always possible to dismiss every report without adequate data. The correct interpretation depends on the quality of evidence available.

In the context of [UFOs and nuclear weapons]({{ 'ufos-and-nuclear-weapons/' | relative_url }}), sensor limitations explain why some historical incidents remain debated decades later. They also explain why modern investigations increasingly prioritise better measurements rather than relying only on dramatic accounts or isolated recordings. The strongest future evidence will likely come not from more unusual sightings, but from better-controlled observations where multiple independent sensors capture the same event.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://science.nasa.gov/uap/faqs/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: science.nasa.gov">[NASA Science]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">science.nasa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Science UAP FAQs</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NASA ScienceUAP FAQs - NASA ScienceMay 8, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: May 8, 2026</span></span></span>
