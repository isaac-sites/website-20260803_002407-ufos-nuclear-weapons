## Introduction

Military training can make a person a better observer, but it does not make observation error impossible. Pilots, missile officers, radar operators and other military personnel are often highly skilled at recognising aircraft, assessing threats and operating complex equipment. That expertise gives their reports importance, especially in sensitive environments such as [nuclear weapons]({{ 'nuclear-weapons/' | relative_url }}) facilities. However, identifying that something unusual was seen is different from proving what caused it.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2023/09/uap-independent-study-team-final-report-0.pdf?emrc=674e2bf25bdc7" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nasa.gov">[NASA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nasa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">uap independent study team final report 0</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UNIDENTIFIED ANOMALOUS PHENOMENA...</span></span></span>

The central issue in debates about military UFO witnesses is therefore not whether trained personnel are credible. A pilot may accurately report an object’s appearance, movement or location while still misjudging distance, speed, size or identity. Human perception, memory and interpretation remain vulnerable to error even among experienced professionals. Research into eyewitness reliability has shown that confidence and expertise do not automatically eliminate mistakes in visual judgement or later recollection.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nationalacademies.org/news/using-science-to-improve-eyewitness-testimony" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nationalacademies.org">[National Academies]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nationalacademies.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Academies Using Science to Improve Eyewitness Testimony</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National AcademiesUsing Science to Improve Eyewitness TestimonyJanuary 24, 2020...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 24, 2020</span></span></span>

## What military observers are trained to identify

Military training provides observers with advantages that ordinary witnesses often lack. Aircrew learn aircraft recognition, flight characteristics, navigation, instrument interpretation and threat assessment. Personnel working around nuclear systems are trained to follow strict procedures, recognise equipment states and respond to anomalies. These skills can improve the quality of an initial report because the observer may record details such as direction, timing, environmental conditions and technical behaviour.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2023/09/uap-independent-study-team-final-report-0.pdf?emrc=674e2bf25bdc7" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nasa.gov">[NASA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nasa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">uap independent study team final report 0</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UNIDENTIFIED ANOMALOUS PHENOMENA...</span></span></span>

This is why military accounts occupy a prominent place in UFO and UAP discussions connected to nuclear weapons. A report from a missile officer or military aviator is not treated the same way as an unverified report of a distant light. The observer may have relevant experience and access to operational information unavailable to the public.

However, training is usually specialised rather than universal. A fighter pilot is trained to understand aircraft performance, not necessarily to estimate the exact range of an unfamiliar object without supporting instruments. A radar operator may understand a sensor display but still face uncertainty caused by [sensor limitations]({{ 'sensor-limitations/' | relative_url }}), atmospheric effects or incomplete information. Expertise improves the quality of observations, but it does not automatically provide the missing data needed to explain an event.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nasa.gov/news-release/update-nasa-shares-uap-independent-study-report-names-director/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nasa.gov">[NASA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nasa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">UPDATE: NASA Shares UAP Independent Study Report; Names Director</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UPDATE: NASA Shares UAP Independent Study Report; Names Director - NASASeptember 14, 2023...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: September 14, 2023</span></span></span>

## Where expert perception can fail

### Distance and speed are difficult to judge visually

One of the biggest challenges in military sightings is that humans are poor at estimating the size, distance and speed of unfamiliar objects without reliable reference points. A small object close to an aircraft can appear to move extremely quickly, while a distant object can appear almost stationary. Changes in viewing angle, aircraft motion and atmospheric conditions can create misleading impressions.

This matters because many unusual UFO reports focus on apparent performance: sudden acceleration, extreme manoeuvres or impossible speeds. Those descriptions may represent genuine observations of unusual-looking events, but converting an appearance into a measured physical performance requires additional information such as accurate range data, calibrated sensors and independent tracking. NASA’s UAP study highlighted this problem, noting that many observations lack the high-quality data required for firm conclusions.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nasa.gov/news-release/update-nasa-shares-uap-independent-study-report-names-director/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nasa.gov">[NASA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nasa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">UPDATE: NASA Shares UAP Independent Study Report; Names Director</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UPDATE: NASA Shares UAP Independent Study Report; Names Director - NASASeptember 14, 2023...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: September 14, 2023</span></span></span>

### Sensors also have limits

Military observers are often associated with advanced technology, but sensors are not perfect. Radar, infrared cameras and optical systems each measure different parts of an event and can produce misleading results when the surrounding information is incomplete.

A sensor may detect a real object while leaving unanswered questions about its distance, size or identity. A video system may capture an unusual shape without providing enough information to calculate its motion. A radar return may represent an object, atmospheric effect or another technical issue requiring further analysis.

The Pentagon’s All-domain Anomaly Resolution Office (AARO) has emphasised that many UAP cases remain unresolved because available information is insufficient, not because the observations necessarily demonstrate unknown technology. Its historical review found no evidence that US government investigations had confirmed [extraterrestrial]({{ 'extraterrestrial/' | relative_url }}) technology as the explanation for UAP reports.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://uap-archive.org/uap/learn/aaro-historical-record-report-findings/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: uap-archive.org">[UAP Records Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">uap-archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">aaro historical record report findings</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UAP Records ArchiveWhat the AARO Historical Record Report Found (2024) &#124; UAP Records Archive...</span></span></span>

### Memory can strengthen confidence without improving accuracy

Military witnesses often describe events years or decades after they occurred, particularly in historical nuclear-related cases. Long-term consistency can be meaningful, but memory does not function as a permanent recording. Psychological research has shown that people can become confident in interpretations that were shaped by later information, discussions or expectations.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nationalacademies.org/news/using-science-to-improve-eyewitness-testimony" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nationalacademies.org">[National Academies]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nationalacademies.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Academies Using Science to Improve Eyewitness Testimony</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">National AcademiesUsing Science to Improve Eyewitness TestimonyJanuary 24, 2020...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 24, 2020</span></span></span>

This creates an important distinction in evaluating testimony:

* A witness may be highly reliable about **having experienced an event**.
* The same witness may be less reliable about **the cause of that event**.

In UFO debates, these two questions are often combined. A trained officer saying “I saw an unidentified object” is evidence that an unusual observation occurred. It is not automatically evidence that the object was a particular type of technology or that it affected nuclear systems.

## Examples of observation versus explanation

### The Malmstrom missile reports

The 1967 missile incidents at Malmstrom Air Force Base remain among the most discussed cases involving military witnesses and nuclear weapons. Former missile personnel have described temporary [missile failures]({{ 'nuclear-missile/' | relative_url }}) associated with reports of unusual objects in the area. Supporters of the case argue that missile officers were experienced professionals who understood normal operations and would have noticed something unusual.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://uap-archive.org/uap/records/aaro-historical-record-report-vol1/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: uap-archive.org">[UAP Records Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">uap-archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on uap-archive.org.</span></span></span>

The observation and explanation, however, are separate questions. The reports show that military personnel described unusual events involving missile facilities. They do not by themselves establish that an unknown object caused the equipment problems. ### U-2 and other secret aircraft sightings

Cold War history provides a clear example of why trained observers can misidentify unfamiliar objects. During the development of high-altitude reconnaissance aircraft such as the U-2 and later programmes, unusual aircraft characteristics created sightings that were initially difficult to explain publicly. ### Modern military UAP encounters

Recent military pilot reports have increased attention on UAP because they often involve multiple sources, including aircraft sensors and trained observers. These cases are more valuable than isolated eyewitness accounts because they may contain additional data. However, even modern cases frequently depend on incomplete records. NASA has argued that systematic collection, sensor calibration and multiple measurements are necessary before strong conclusions can be drawn.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nasa.gov/news-release/update-nasa-shares-uap-independent-study-report-names-director/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nasa.gov">[NASA]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nasa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">UPDATE: NASA Shares UAP Independent Study Report; Names Director</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UPDATE: NASA Shares UAP Independent Study Report; Names Director - NASASeptember 14, 2023...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: September 14, 2023</span></span></span>

## Why expertise still matters, but cannot settle the debate


<div class="content-callout content-callout--key content-callout--takeaway" markdown="1">

Military training changes the quality of a UFO report, but not the basic limits of human observation. A trained witness can provide valuable information about what happened: where an event occurred, what was seen, how long it lasted and what systems were involved. That information can help investigators determine whether an incident was a known aircraft, a sensor issue, an atmospheric effect or something that remains unidentified.

</div>

The mistake is assuming that expertise transforms an observation into an explanation. Military professionals are trained to recognise threats and operate equipment, not to eliminate every possible perceptual or analytical error. The strongest interpretation of military UFO testimony is therefore balanced: trained observers deserve serious consideration, especially in nuclear-related cases, but their expertise must be combined with physical evidence, reliable measurements and independent analysis before conclusions about cause can be drawn.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://uap-archive.org/uap/learn/aaro-historical-record-report-findings/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: uap-archive.org">[UAP Records Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">uap-archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">aaro historical record report findings</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UAP Records ArchiveWhat the AARO Historical Record Report Found (2024) &#124; UAP Records Archive...</span></span></span>
