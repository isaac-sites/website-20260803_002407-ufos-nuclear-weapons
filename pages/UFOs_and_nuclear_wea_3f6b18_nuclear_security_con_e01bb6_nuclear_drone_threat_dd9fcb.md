## Introduction

Drones have changed the security problem around nuclear sites by making it harder to answer a basic but critical question: what is approaching the facility, and what does it intend to do? Unlike traditional aircraft, small uncrewed aerial systems (UAS) can be inexpensive, difficult to detect at long range, and capable of operating close to sensitive areas. For nuclear facilities, the concern is not that every unidentified object represents an extraordinary threat, but that an unknown aerial presence can create uncertainty about surveillance, sabotage, safety, or hostile intent. The detection challenge sits at the centre of modern [nuclear security]({{ 'security-concerns-0719dd/' | relative_url }}) because operators must identify genuine risks while avoiding false alarms caused by ordinary aircraft, birds, weather effects, or authorised activity.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nrc.gov/reading-rm/doc-collections/fact-sheets/fs-drone-pwr-plant-security" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nrc.gov">[Nuclear Regulatory Commission]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nrc.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Nuclear Regulatory CommissionDrones and Nuclear Power Plant Security &#124; Nuclear Regulatory CommissionJanuary 21, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 21, 2025</span></span></span>

The rise of drones adds a new layer to the wider issue of unidentified aerial activity near nuclear-related locations. Historically, unusual sightings around nuclear sites have often been discussed through the lens of unexplained phenomena, but modern drone incidents show a more conventional security challenge: small airborne systems can test the ability of highly protected facilities to detect, classify, and respond quickly.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nrc.gov/reading-rm/doc-collections/fact-sheets/fs-drone-pwr-plant-security" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nrc.gov">[Nuclear Regulatory Commission]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nrc.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Nuclear Regulatory CommissionDrones and Nuclear Power Plant Security &#124; Nuclear Regulatory CommissionJanuary 21, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 21, 2025</span></span></span>

## Drone risks around sensitive facilities

Nuclear facilities are designed around layered security: physical barriers, surveillance, access controls, trained security personnel, and procedures for responding to threats. However, these systems were traditionally built with ground intrusion and larger aircraft threats in mind. Small drones introduce a different type of problem because they can approach from unexpected directions, remain difficult to classify, and potentially carry sensors or other equipment without needing a human pilot near the target.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nrc.gov/security" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nrc.gov">[Nuclear Regulatory Commission]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nrc.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on nrc.gov.</span></span></span>

The security concern is not limited to direct physical damage. A drone near a nuclear site could be used for intelligence collection by recording layouts, security routines, or operational activity. It could also create disruption by forcing security teams to investigate an unknown object or by generating uncertainty during a sensitive operational period. Research on countering civilian drone threats at nuclear facilities has highlighted that effective protection requires both detection systems and a coordinated response architecture rather than relying on a single technology.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1874548217300124" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: sciencedirect.com">[ScienceDirect]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">sciencedirect.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ScienceDirect A conceptual system architecture for countering the civilian unmanned aerial vehicles threat to nuclear facilities</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">ScienceDirectA conceptual system architecture for countering the civilian unmanned aerial vehicles threat to nuclear facilities - Science...</span></span></span>

Recent regulatory attention reflects this changing risk environment. In the United States, the Nuclear Regulatory Commission (NRC) has required nuclear power plant operators to report drone sightings over facilities, with reports shared with agencies including the Federal Aviation Administration, Federal Bureau of Investigation, and law enforcement partners. The NRC has also noted that nuclear plants maintain existing security measures, including surveillance and intrusion detection systems, but that drone activity requires continued assessment.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nrc.gov/reading-rm/doc-collections/fact-sheets/fs-drone-pwr-plant-security" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nrc.gov">[Nuclear Regulatory Commission]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nrc.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Nuclear Regulatory CommissionDrones and Nuclear Power Plant Security &#124; Nuclear Regulatory CommissionJanuary 21, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 21, 2025</span></span></span>

The challenge is especially visible in conflict environments, where drones have become a regular feature of modern warfare. The International Atomic Energy Agency (IAEA) has repeatedly highlighted the nuclear safety implications of military activity involving drones near nuclear facilities, including incidents around Ukrainian nuclear sites. ## Detection and identification problems

Detecting a drone is only the first step. The harder task is determining what the object is, whether it is authorised, and whether it represents a threat. Small drones may have a limited radar signature, operate at low altitude, and move in ways that make them difficult to separate from harmless objects. A security system that detects every possible airborne object may generate too many false alarms, while a system designed to avoid false positives may miss a genuine intrusion.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2111.03760" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: arxiv.org">[arXiv]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">arxiv.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">arXiv Securing your Airspace: Detection of Drones Trespassing Protected Areas</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">arXiv Securing your Airspace: Detection of Drones Trespassing Protected Areas</span></span></span>

Different detection methods reveal different parts of the problem:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--benefit" markdown="1">

* **Radar detection** can provide distance and movement information but may struggle with very small, low-flying objects.
* **Optical and infrared sensors** can help identify objects visually but depend on conditions such as lighting, weather, and line of sight.
* **Radio-frequency monitoring** can detect communications between a drone and operator, but autonomous drones or unusual control methods may reduce this advantage.
* **Acoustic detection** can identify drone sounds but can be affected by environmental noise.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2111.03760" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: arxiv.org">[arXiv]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">arxiv.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">arXiv Securing your Airspace: Detection of Drones Trespassing Protected Areas</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">arXiv Securing your Airspace: Detection of Drones Trespassing Protected Areas</span></span></span>

</div>

Because each method has weaknesses, researchers have increasingly examined combined approaches that use multiple sensors together. Studies of protected-area drone detection have found that combining radar, acoustic, optical, and radio-frequency information can improve classification compared with relying on a single source of data.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2111.03760" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: arxiv.org">[arXiv]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">arxiv.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">arXiv Securing your Airspace: Detection of Drones Trespassing Protected Areas</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">arXiv Securing your Airspace: Detection of Drones Trespassing Protected Areas</span></span></span>

Identification is also complicated by the difference between an unknown object and a hostile object. A drone may be flown by a hobbyist violating [airspace rules]({{ 'airspace-rules/' | relative_url }}), a journalist seeking footage, a contractor conducting authorised work, or an actor attempting surveillance. From the perspective of a nuclear security team, the immediate challenge is managing uncertainty: the facility must treat the situation seriously enough to protect against threats while gathering enough information to avoid unnecessary escalation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nrc.gov/reading-rm/doc-collections/fact-sheets/fs-drone-pwr-plant-security" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nrc.gov">[Nuclear Regulatory Commission]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nrc.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Nuclear Regulatory CommissionDrones and Nuclear Power Plant Security &#124; Nuclear Regulatory CommissionJanuary 21, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 21, 2025</span></span></span>

This problem connects directly to broader debates about unidentified aerial phenomena near nuclear locations. A reported object does not need an unusual explanation to matter from a security perspective. A misidentified aircraft, unauthorised drone, or intelligence-gathering platform can create the same operational challenge: defenders must establish what happened before deciding how to respond.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nrc.gov/reading-rm/doc-collections/fact-sheets/fs-drone-pwr-plant-security" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nrc.gov">[Nuclear Regulatory Commission]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nrc.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Nuclear Regulatory CommissionDrones and Nuclear Power Plant Security &#124; Nuclear Regulatory CommissionJanuary 21, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 21, 2025</span></span></span>

## Security responses and lessons

The main lesson from drone incidents at nuclear sites is that detection cannot be separated from response planning. A facility needs systems that allow operators to move from observation to assessment and, where necessary, coordinated action with aviation authorities, intelligence organisations, and law enforcement. The NRC’s reporting requirements illustrate this approach by connecting nuclear operators with wider government monitoring and response networks.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nrc.gov/reading-rm/doc-collections/fact-sheets/fs-drone-pwr-plant-security" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nrc.gov">[Nuclear Regulatory Commission]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nrc.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Nuclear Regulatory CommissionDrones and Nuclear Power Plant Security &#124; Nuclear Regulatory CommissionJanuary 21, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 21, 2025</span></span></span>

Counter-drone strategies generally focus on three stages:

1. **Detect** — identify that an airborne object is present.
2. **Track and classify** — determine its location, behaviour, and likely purpose.

3. **Respond** — apply appropriate measures while considering safety, legal authority, and the surrounding environment. [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1874548217300124)

A major difficulty is that nuclear facilities are not always free to use every possible countermeasure. Interfering with a drone through electronic methods, physically capturing it, or forcing it down can create separate risks, including interference with other communications or uncertainty about where a disabled drone may land. Security responses therefore have to balance speed with control. [Nuclear Regulatory Commission](https://www.nrc.gov/reading-rm/doc-collections/fact-sheets/fs-drone-pwr-plant-security)

The wider significance for the UFO-and-[nuclear-weapons]({{ 'nuclear-weapons/' | relative_url }}) debate is that drones provide a concrete example of why unidentified aerial activity around nuclear assets matters. The key issue is not whether an unknown object has an extraordinary origin, but whether security systems can reliably detect, understand, and manage unfamiliar activity near highly sensitive locations. Modern drones demonstrate that even ordinary technology can challenge assumptions about visibility, identification, and response time in nuclear security.
