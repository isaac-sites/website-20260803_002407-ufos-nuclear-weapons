## Introduction

Nuclear plant drone sightings have become a distinct security issue because an unidentified object near a protected facility can quickly become an operational question, even when the object is later assessed as an ordinary uncrewed aircraft. The central challenge for operators is not simply identifying whether something in the air is a drone, but deciding whether it represents a safety concern, a security threat, an illegal flight, or a mistaken observation. Nuclear facilities therefore treat reported drone activity as an event requiring assessment, documentation, and coordination rather than automatically as evidence of hostile action.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nrc.gov/reading-rm/doc-collections/fact-sheets/fs-drone-pwr-plant-security" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nrc.gov">[Nuclear Regulatory Commission]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nrc.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Nuclear Regulatory CommissionDrones and Nuclear Power Plant Security &#124; Nuclear Regulatory CommissionJanuary 21, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 21, 2025</span></span></span>

## Reported drone incidents at facilities

### Why sightings trigger attention even without confirmed threats

Commercial nuclear power plants operate under strict security requirements, making their surrounding airspace a sensitive environment. The growth of inexpensive and capable uncrewed aircraft systems (UAS) has created a new category of incidents: objects that may be easy to acquire and operate but difficult to immediately identify from the ground. A small drone seen near a protected area may raise questions about the operator, purpose, flight path, and whether the activity was accidental or deliberate.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nrc.gov/reading-rm/doc-collections/fact-sheets/fs-drone-pwr-plant-security" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nrc.gov">[Nuclear Regulatory Commission]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nrc.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Nuclear Regulatory CommissionDrones and Nuclear Power Plant Security &#124; Nuclear Regulatory CommissionJanuary 21, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 21, 2025</span></span></span>

The Nuclear Regulatory Commission (NRC) has reported that increasing availability of commercial drones has resulted in numerous sightings over critical infrastructure, including nuclear power plants. In response, US nuclear regulations were updated to require nuclear power plant licensees to report drone sightings over their facilities, with reports shared with agencies including the NRC, Federal Aviation Administration (FAA), Federal Bureau of Investigation (FBI), and local law enforcement.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nrc.gov/reading-rm/doc-collections/fact-sheets/fs-drone-pwr-plant-security" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nrc.gov">[Nuclear Regulatory Commission]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nrc.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Nuclear Regulatory CommissionDrones and Nuclear Power Plant Security &#124; Nuclear Regulatory CommissionJanuary 21, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 21, 2025</span></span></span>

These reports do not automatically indicate that a plant has been compromised. A sighting may involve a hobby aircraft, a commercial drone, an authorised flight, or an object that is misidentified at a distance. The security significance comes from the location and uncertainty: a drone over a sensitive facility must be assessed because the same technology can range from harmless photography to unauthorised surveillance.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nrc.gov/reading-rm/doc-collections/fact-sheets/fs-drone-pwr-plant-security" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nrc.gov">[Nuclear Regulatory Commission]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nrc.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Nuclear Regulatory CommissionDrones and Nuclear Power Plant Security &#124; Nuclear Regulatory CommissionJanuary 21, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 21, 2025</span></span></span>

### Documented patterns and difficult assessments

Drone incidents around nuclear facilities have often involved incomplete information. Witnesses may see lights at night, estimate distance incorrectly, or observe an aircraft without knowing its size or altitude. Security teams must therefore separate the initial report from the final assessment. A report of a “large drone” may later become a smaller consumer aircraft viewed under poor conditions, while some cases remain unresolved because there is insufficient [sensor data]({{ 'sensor-data/' | relative_url }}) or no identified operator.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.faa.gov/uas/public_safety_gov/sightings_reports" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: faa.gov">[Federal Aviation Administration]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">faa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on faa.gov.</span></span></span>

This uncertainty is one reason drone incidents sometimes enter broader UAP discussions. A sighting can begin as an unknown object but later be classified as a drone, aircraft, balloon, or another conventional explanation. The unresolved category reflects a lack of identification at the time of reporting rather than proof of an unusual technology.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nrc.gov/reading-rm/doc-collections/fact-sheets/fs-drone-pwr-plant-security" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nrc.gov">[Nuclear Regulatory Commission]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nrc.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Nuclear Regulatory CommissionDrones and Nuclear Power Plant Security &#124; Nuclear Regulatory CommissionJanuary 21, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 21, 2025</span></span></span>

## Operator response and coordination

### What happens after a drone is reported

A nuclear plant’s response generally begins with information gathering. Operators and security personnel seek to establish basic facts: where the object was seen, when it appeared, how it moved, whether it entered restricted areas, and whether cameras, radar, or other systems recorded additional information. The aim is to create a reliable picture before deciding whether the event requires escalation.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nrc.gov/reading-rm/doc-collections/fact-sheets/fs-drone-pwr-plant-security" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nrc.gov">[Nuclear Regulatory Commission]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nrc.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Nuclear Regulatory CommissionDrones and Nuclear Power Plant Security &#124; Nuclear Regulatory CommissionJanuary 21, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 21, 2025</span></span></span>

A typical response pathway includes:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--comparison" markdown="1">

* **Detection and assessment:** Security personnel record observations and compare them with available surveillance information.
* **Notification and reporting:** Required reports are made to relevant authorities according to regulatory requirements.
* **Coordination:** Nuclear operators work with agencies responsible for airspace, law enforcement, and security response.
* **Follow-up analysis:** Information is reviewed to determine whether the event was accidental, unauthorised, suspicious, or unresolved.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nrc.gov/reading-rm/doc-collections/fact-sheets/fs-drone-pwr-plant-security" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nrc.gov">[Nuclear Regulatory Commission]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nrc.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Nuclear Regulatory CommissionDrones and Nuclear Power Plant Security &#124; Nuclear Regulatory CommissionJanuary 21, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 21, 2025</span></span></span>

</div>

The FAA’s guidance for drone incidents similarly emphasises collecting practical details, including the drone’s appearance, behaviour, location, and possible operator, while coordinating with appropriate authorities. These procedures reflect a broader principle: identifying the aircraft and the person controlling it is often more useful than focusing only on the object itself.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.faa.gov/uas/public_safety_gov/sightings_reports" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: faa.gov">[Federal Aviation Administration]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">faa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on faa.gov.</span></span></span>

### Why operators do not simply shoot drones down

A common public assumption is that nuclear facilities can immediately disable any drone entering restricted airspace. In practice, authority over counter-drone actions is more complicated. The NRC notes that commercial nuclear plant security forces do not have authority to interdict or shoot down aircraft, including drones, flying over their facilities. Response decisions must fit within legal authorities and coordinated government procedures.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nrc.gov/reading-rm/doc-collections/fact-sheets/fs-drone-pwr-plant-security" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nrc.gov">[Nuclear Regulatory Commission]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nrc.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Nuclear Regulatory CommissionDrones and Nuclear Power Plant Security &#124; Nuclear Regulatory CommissionJanuary 21, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 21, 2025</span></span></span>

Instead, nuclear facilities rely on layered security. Plants maintain physical protection measures, trained security forces, surveillance systems, and emergency response arrangements designed around a range of possible threats. The presence of a drone therefore adds a detection and coordination challenge rather than replacing existing security systems.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nrc.gov/reading-rm/doc-collections/fact-sheets/fs-drone-pwr-plant-security" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nrc.gov">[Nuclear Regulatory Commission]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nrc.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Nuclear Regulatory CommissionDrones and Nuclear Power Plant Security &#124; Nuclear Regulatory CommissionJanuary 21, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 21, 2025</span></span></span>

## Separating threat from mistaken observation

### The identification problem

The most important distinction in nuclear plant drone cases is between an unknown observation and a confirmed threat. A drone sighting answers only that someone observed something believed to be an uncrewed aircraft. It does not by itself establish who operated it, why it was present, or whether it could affect plant safety.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nrc.gov/reading-rm/doc-collections/fact-sheets/fs-drone-pwr-plant-security" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nrc.gov">[Nuclear Regulatory Commission]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nrc.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Nuclear Regulatory CommissionDrones and Nuclear Power Plant Security &#124; Nuclear Regulatory CommissionJanuary 21, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 21, 2025</span></span></span>

Several factors complicate identification:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **Distance and lighting:** Small drones can appear larger or behave differently than they actually do when viewed from far away or at night.
* **Limited evidence:** A brief visual sighting may provide little information about the object’s capabilities.
* **Mixed aircraft environment:** Normal aircraft, helicopters, satellites, balloons, and drones can create overlapping observations.
* **Unknown intent:** Even a confirmed drone may leave unanswered questions about whether the flight was recreational, commercial, accidental, or deliberate.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.faa.gov/uas/public_safety_gov/sightings_reports" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: faa.gov">[Federal Aviation Administration]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">faa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on faa.gov.</span></span></span>

</div>

This distinction is especially important in discussions linking nuclear sites with UAP reports. A facility receiving an unusual aerial report does not mean the object represents an unexplained technology. In many cases, the practical security question is whether the event can be attributed to known aircraft activity or whether additional investigation is needed.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nrc.gov/reading-rm/doc-collections/fact-sheets/fs-drone-pwr-plant-security" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nrc.gov">[Nuclear Regulatory Commission]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nrc.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Nuclear Regulatory CommissionDrones and Nuclear Power Plant Security &#124; Nuclear Regulatory CommissionJanuary 21, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 21, 2025</span></span></span>

### The evolving role of counter-drone security

As drones become more common, nuclear and other high-security facilities are developing more sophisticated approaches to detection and response. International [nuclear security]({{ 'security-concerns-0719dd/' | relative_url }}) guidance has examined the use of unmanned systems, including the need for better situational awareness and assessment capabilities around protected facilities.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://nucleus.iaea.org/sites/nss-oui/Published%20Chunks/m_1d1a11b6-2571-4c8e-a379-08519ef8fbb8/c_224f95f0-0448-4d8e-bc53-4dcccdf3fcd3__2_0.Html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nucleus.iaea.org">[Nucleus]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nucleus.iaea.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NSS No. 40-T Handbook on the Design of Physical Protection Systems for Nuclear Material and Nuclear Facilities - Appendix - Exampl...</span></span></span>

The challenge for operators is balancing improved awareness with proportionate response. Too little attention could allow genuine security concerns to go unnoticed, while treating every uncertain sighting as a major threat could overwhelm security resources.
