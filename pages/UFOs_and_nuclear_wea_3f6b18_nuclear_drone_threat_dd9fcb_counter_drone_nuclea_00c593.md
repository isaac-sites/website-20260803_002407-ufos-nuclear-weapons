## Introduction

Nuclear sites increasingly treat drones as a security problem that requires more than a single interception device. The modern approach to counter-drone protection combines early detection, tracking, decision-making, communication with authorities, and carefully controlled response options. The aim is not simply to “shoot down” every aircraft near a facility, but to determine whether a drone is accidental, suspicious, or hostile while protecting a site’s operations and surrounding communities.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nrc.gov/reading-rm/doc-collections/fact-sheets/fs-drone-pwr-plant-security" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nrc.gov">[Nuclear Regulatory Commission]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nrc.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Nuclear Regulatory CommissionDrones and Nuclear Power Plant Security &#124; Nuclear Regulatory CommissionJanuary 21, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 21, 2025</span></span></span>

This layered approach has become especially important because small unmanned aircraft are inexpensive, widely available, and difficult to manage with traditional security methods alone. Nuclear regulators and international security bodies increasingly view counter-uncrewed aircraft systems (C-UAS) as part of a broader physical protection strategy, alongside established measures such as barriers, surveillance, and trained security forces.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nrc.gov/reading-rm/doc-collections/fact-sheets/fs-drone-pwr-plant-security" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nrc.gov">[Nuclear Regulatory Commission]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nrc.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Nuclear Regulatory CommissionDrones and Nuclear Power Plant Security &#124; Nuclear Regulatory CommissionJanuary 21, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 21, 2025</span></span></span>

## Detecting small unmanned aircraft

The first challenge for nuclear facilities is knowing what is approaching the site. Small drones can be difficult to detect because they may fly at low altitude, have small radar signatures, and operate in environments where birds, weather, and other aircraft create false alarms. For this reason, modern counter-drone systems usually combine several detection methods rather than relying on one sensor.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2008.12461" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: arxiv.org">[arXiv]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">arxiv.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Counter-Unmanned Aircraft System(s) (C-UAS): State of the Art, Challenges and Future TrendsAugust 28, 2020...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 28, 2020</span></span></span>

Common detection layers include:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--metric" markdown="1">

* **Radar systems** that identify movement and track airborne objects.
* **Radio-frequency sensors** that detect communications between a drone and its operator.
* **Optical and infrared cameras** that provide visual confirmation.
* **Acoustic sensors** that recognise drone sound patterns.
* **Data-fusion systems** that combine information from multiple sensors to improve identification.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2008.12461" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: arxiv.org">[arXiv]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">arxiv.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Counter-Unmanned Aircraft System(s) (C-UAS): State of the Art, Challenges and Future TrendsAugust 28, 2020...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 28, 2020</span></span></span>

</div>

For nuclear facilities, detection is only the first step. A security team must understand what the drone is doing, where it came from, and whether it presents a credible threat. A small aircraft operated by a confused hobbyist near restricted airspace requires a different response from a deliberately operated drone conducting surveillance or attempting to interfere with a protected site. The U.S. National [Nuclear Security]({{ 'security-concerns-0719dd/' | relative_url }}) Administration (NNSA) describes its counter-UAS approach around detection, assessment, tracking, and coordinated response rather than automatic engagement.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.energy.gov/nnsa/protecting-nnsa-facilities-counter-unmanned-aircraft-systems" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: energy.gov">[The Department of Energy&#x27;s Energy.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">energy.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on energy.gov.</span></span></span>

This distinction matters because nuclear sites operate under strict safety requirements. A defensive action that disrupts a drone could create new risks if it causes debris to fall onto sensitive equipment, affects nearby infrastructure, or interferes with legitimate aviation activity. Counter-drone protection is therefore designed as a controlled security process, not simply an air-defence reaction.

## Response options and legal limits

Once a drone is detected and assessed, facilities need ways to reduce the threat. These options generally fall into two categories: **soft-kill measures**, which disrupt or disable the drone without physically destroying it, and **hard-kill measures**, which physically capture or destroy the aircraft.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/countering-drone-threats-to-shipping/countering-drone-threats-to-shipping" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: GOV.UK">[GOV.UK]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">GOV.UK</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Countering drone threats to shipping</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">20, 2024...</span></span></span>

Soft-kill approaches can include electronic measures that interfere with a drone’s communication links or navigation systems. These methods may allow security teams to force a drone to land or prevent it from continuing toward a protected area. However, their effectiveness depends on the drone’s design. More autonomous systems, alternative communication methods, or systems that do not rely on standard radio links can reduce the usefulness of some electronic countermeasures.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://arxiv.org/abs/2008.12461" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: arxiv.org">[arXiv]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">arxiv.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Counter-Unmanned Aircraft System(s) (C-UAS): State of the Art, Challenges and Future TrendsAugust 28, 2020...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: August 28, 2020</span></span></span>

Physical response options include capture systems, interceptor drones, or other methods designed to remove the aircraft from the protected area. These approaches can provide investigators with the drone itself, which may help determine whether an incident was accidental or deliberate. However, physical interception must be balanced against the risks of falling debris and the difficulty of operating safely near nuclear infrastructure.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://apnews.com/article/5189a0f69c8f8e25ef1e41faa53bfecb" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: apnews.com">[AP News]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">apnews.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">These unmanned aerial vehicles pose threats at sensitive locations like nuclear plants, airports, and public events, and have even been u...</span></span></span>

A major challenge is that nuclear operators do not always have unlimited legal authority to act against aircraft. In the United States, for example, the Nuclear Regulatory Commission states that commercial nuclear power plant security forces do not have authority to independently interdict or shoot down aircraft over their facilities. Instead, drone incidents involve coordination with agencies including the Federal Aviation Administration, Federal Bureau of Investigation, and law enforcement authorities.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nrc.gov/reading-rm/doc-collections/fact-sheets/fs-drone-pwr-plant-security" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nrc.gov">[Nuclear Regulatory Commission]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nrc.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Nuclear Regulatory CommissionDrones and Nuclear Power Plant Security &#124; Nuclear Regulatory CommissionJanuary 21, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 21, 2025</span></span></span>

For sensitive government nuclear facilities, authorities may have broader powers. U.S. law provides the Department of Energy with specific authority to take actions against unmanned aircraft threats affecting certain nuclear facilities and assets.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?edition=prelim&amp;num=0&amp;req=granuleid%3AUSC-prelim-title10-section6227" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: uscode.house.gov">[U.S. Code]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">uscode.house.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">July 26, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: July 26, 2026</span></span></span> The difference illustrates a wider issue in counter-drone security: technology can detect and sometimes defeat drones, but legal frameworks determine when and how those capabilities can be used.

## Why nuclear sites favour layered defence

A nuclear facility’s counter-drone system is designed around uncertainty. Security planners cannot assume that every drone sighting represents an attack, but they also cannot ignore repeated or unexplained activity around sensitive infrastructure. The practical solution is a chain of protection: detect the aircraft, identify its behaviour, assess the risk, coordinate the response, and preserve evidence where possible.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.energy.gov/nnsa/protecting-nnsa-facilities-counter-unmanned-aircraft-systems" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: energy.gov">[The Department of Energy&#x27;s Energy.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">energy.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on energy.gov.</span></span></span>

The need for this layered model became clearer after drone-related incidents around nuclear facilities in conflict zones. At Ukraine’s Zaporizhzhia Nuclear Power Plant, the International Atomic Energy Agency has repeatedly warned that drone activity near nuclear installations creates safety and security risks, even when critical nuclear systems are not damaged.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.reuters.com/business/energy/iaea-says-drone-damaged-equipment-zaporizhzhia-nuclear-plant-ukraine-2026-05-05/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: reuters.com">[Reuters]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">reuters.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">IAEA says drone damaged equipment at Zaporizhzhia nuclear plant in Ukraine</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The plant, Europe&#x27;s largest nuclear facility, has been under Russian control since early in the 2022 invasion. Amid ongoing conflict near...</span></span></span> These events demonstrate that drones can become part of wider security crises by increasing uncertainty, creating opportunities for miscalculation, and placing pressure on emergency response systems.

The lesson for civilian nuclear operators is not that every facility requires military-style air defence. Instead, it is that existing physical security arrangements must adapt to a new category of low-cost airborne threat. A drone may not have the destructive capability of a conventional weapon, but it can still challenge surveillance, confidentiality, emergency planning, and public confidence.

## Future security approaches

Future nuclear-site protection is likely to focus on making counter-drone systems more integrated, automated, and adaptable. The International Atomic Energy Agency has highlighted the difficulty of preparing for rapidly changing drone capabilities, including longer-range systems, higher payload capacity, autonomous operation, and coordinated drone groups.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://conferences.iaea.org/event/349/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: conferences.iaea.org">[IAEA Conferences]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">conferences.iaea.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">IAEA ConferencesTechnical Meeting on Nuclear Security Countermeasures for Uncrewed Aerial Vehicles (30 October 2023 - 3 November 2023): O...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: October 2023</span></span></span>

Several developments are shaping this next phase:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* **Better sensor integration:** Facilities are moving towards systems that combine radar, cameras, radio monitoring, and software analysis into a single operational picture.
* **Improved identification:** Artificial intelligence and automated tracking tools may help distinguish legitimate aircraft, wildlife, and suspicious drones more quickly.
* **Closer coordination:** Nuclear operators, aviation authorities, intelligence agencies, and law enforcement are increasingly expected to share information during incidents.
* **Flexible regulations:** Governments face the challenge of allowing effective counter-drone action while preventing unnecessary interference with aviation safety and civil rights.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.energy.gov/nnsa/protecting-nnsa-facilities-counter-unmanned-aircraft-systems" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: energy.gov">[The Department of Energy&#x27;s Energy.gov]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">energy.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on energy.gov.</span></span></span>

The central security question is shifting from whether a facility can stop a single drone to whether it can manage an evolving drone environment. Nuclear sites are therefore building systems that emphasise awareness, verification, and proportionate response. Counter-drone protection is becoming another layer in the long-standing principle of nuclear security: preventing a small vulnerability from becoming a larger safety event.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nrc.gov/reading-rm/doc-collections/fact-sheets/fs-drone-pwr-plant-security" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nrc.gov">[Nuclear Regulatory Commission]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nrc.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Nuclear Regulatory CommissionDrones and Nuclear Power Plant Security &#124; Nuclear Regulatory CommissionJanuary 21, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: January 21, 2025</span></span></span>
