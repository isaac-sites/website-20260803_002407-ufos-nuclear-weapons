## Introduction

Could electrical effects disrupt missiles? Yes, in principle. Missile systems depend on tightly integrated electronics, power supplies, communications links, sensors and control computers, so unwanted electromagnetic energy can interfere with operations if systems are not adequately protected. This does not mean that every unexplained missile failure has an electromagnetic cause, nor does it prove claims that unidentified aerial phenomena (UAP) have disabled [nuclear weapons]({{ 'nuclear-weapons/' | relative_url }}). The stronger technical conclusion is narrower: electromagnetic interference (EMI), electromagnetic pulses (EMP), electrical transients and related effects are recognised engineering risks that missile designers have long tested against.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ntrl.ntis.gov/NTRL/dashboard/searchResults/titleDetail/ADA162577.xhtml" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ntrl.ntis.gov">[NTRL]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ntrl.ntis.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Technical Reports Library</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Electromagnetic Environmental Criteria for US Army Missile Systems: EMC (Electromagnetic Compatibility), EMR (Electromagnetic Radiati...</span></span></span>

## How missile electronics depend on stable systems

Modern missiles are not single machines with one vulnerable switch. They are networks of electronic subsystems that must operate together: guidance equipment determines position and trajectory, computers process commands, power systems provide regulated electricity, and communication circuits carry information between components. A failure in any one of these areas can prevent a missile from performing its mission even if the weapon structure and propulsion system remain intact.

For [nuclear missile]({{ 'nuclear-missile/' | relative_url }}) systems, reliability requirements are particularly demanding because readiness depends on maintaining secure links between launch facilities, command systems and the missile itself. Engineers therefore treat the electromagnetic environment as part of the operating conditions of the weapon system, not as an unusual outside threat. Military electromagnetic compatibility standards exist to control both the unwanted emissions produced by equipment and the ability of systems to resist external interference.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://standards.nasa.gov/standard/NASA/MIL-STD-461" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: standards.nasa.gov">[NASA Standards]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">standards.nasa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NASA StandardsRequirements for the Control of Electromagnetic Interference Characteristics of Subsystems and Equipment &#124; Standards...</span></span></span>

A key distinction is between **electromagnetic compatibility** and **electromagnetic immunity**. Compatibility means that a system does not disrupt nearby equipment through its own electrical emissions. Immunity means that the system continues operating when exposed to external signals, such as radio-frequency energy, static discharge, lightning effects or electromagnetic pulses. Missile programmes have historically included testing against these hazards because even small electrical disturbances can have consequences in complex control systems.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ntrl.ntis.gov/NTRL/dashboard/searchResults/titleDetail/ADA162577.xhtml" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ntrl.ntis.gov">[NTRL]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ntrl.ntis.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">National Technical Reports Library</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Electromagnetic Environmental Criteria for US Army Missile Systems: EMC (Electromagnetic Compatibility), EMR (Electromagnetic Radiati...</span></span></span>

## Possible electromagnetic failure pathways

Electromagnetic effects can disrupt missile systems through several technical pathways. The exact outcome depends on the strength, frequency and duration of the disturbance, as well as the design of the equipment being affected.

One pathway is **electromagnetic interference through wiring and circuits**. Electrical signals travelling through cables can be affected by unwanted currents or voltages induced by nearby electromagnetic fields. A disturbance might create false signals, interrupt communication between components or cause protective systems to enter a fault state. This is why military electronics are designed with shielding, filtering and grounding measures.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://standards.nasa.gov/standard/NASA/MIL-STD-461" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: standards.nasa.gov">[NASA Standards]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">standards.nasa.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">NASA StandardsRequirements for the Control of Electromagnetic Interference Characteristics of Subsystems and Equipment &#124; Standards...</span></span></span>

Another pathway is an **electromagnetic pulse**. A strong pulse can rapidly create electrical stresses in conductors, potentially damaging components or causing temporary malfunctions. High-altitude nuclear explosions are one source of EMP concerns because they can generate large electromagnetic effects over wide areas, but engineers also consider non-nuclear electromagnetic environments such as lightning, radar emissions and other intense sources.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.dhs.gov/science-and-technology/electromagnetic-pulse-empgeomagnetic-disturbance" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: dhs.gov">[Department of Homeland Security]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">dhs.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Department of Homeland SecurityElectromagnetic Pulse (EMP)/Geomagnetic Disturbance &#124; Homeland SecuritySeptember 30, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: September 30, 2025</span></span></span>

A third pathway involves **power system disturbances**. Missile facilities rely on stable electrical supplies, backup systems and monitoring equipment. Voltage spikes, component failures or abnormal electrical conditions can produce faults that appear sudden and widespread, especially in older systems with many interconnected components.

The importance of these pathways is illustrated by historical testing. A 1968 technical report on Minuteman power-control equipment examined electromagnetic interference requirements and susceptibility testing for missile systems, showing that engineers were actively studying how interference could affect missile electronics during the Cold War era.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ntrl.ntis.gov/NTRL/dashboard/searchResults/titleDetail/AD843033.xhtml" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ntrl.ntis.gov">[NTRL]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ntrl.ntis.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Electro-Interference Test Report for the Power Control Set, CTLI/Mod 7/Mod 8, Figure A 9112M. &#124; National Technical Reports Library...</span></span></span>

## The Malmstrom shutdown and the electromagnetic question

The most discussed example in the UFO-and-nuclear-weapons debate is the March 1967 failure of ten Minuteman I missiles at Malmstrom Air Force Base, Montana. The missiles in [Echo Flight]({{ 'echo-flight/' | relative_url }}) became unavailable for alert duty, creating a serious operational concern. Reports from personnel about unusual lights near the facilities later became central to claims that an unidentified object had caused the shutdown.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nicap.org/reports/malmstrom67-2.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nicap.org">[Nicap]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nicap.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">malmstrom67 2</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Malmstrom AFB Missile/UFO Incident, Mar 16, 1967November 27, 1996...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 27, 1996</span></span></span>

The electromagnetic explanation is based on the fact that missile electronics can be vulnerable to carefully introduced electrical disturbances. Some accounts of the Boeing investigation into the incident describe attempts to reproduce the failure by introducing a pulse into a missile-related data line. These accounts have been used by proponents of an external electromagnetic explanation, although they do not demonstrate that such a pulse was actually delivered during the 1967 event.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nicap.org/reports/malmstrom67-2.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nicap.org">[Nicap]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nicap.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">malmstrom67 2</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Malmstrom AFB Missile/UFO Incident, Mar 16, 1967November 27, 1996...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 27, 1996</span></span></span>

The technical challenge is that a real-world electromagnetic disruption would have needed to interact with a highly protected military system in a specific way. A random electrical event could cause problems, but reproducing the exact failure mode and proving the source of the disturbance are separate questions. The existence of a possible mechanism does not by itself establish the cause of a historical incident.

This distinction is important in evaluating extraordinary claims. Missile electronics can fail because of ordinary engineering problems, and electromagnetic effects are one category among several possible explanations. Evidence that a system could theoretically be disrupted is not the same as evidence that it was disrupted by an unknown external technology.

## Why technical causes remain debated

The debate continues partly because Cold War missile incidents involve incomplete public records, complex technology and conflicting interpretations. Some witnesses have argued that the timing of reported aerial phenomena and missile failures suggests a connection. Others emphasise that classified military testing, equipment faults and electrical failures provide more conventional possibilities.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.nicap.org/reports/malmstrom67-2.htm" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: nicap.org">[Nicap]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">nicap.org</span><span class="citation-popover-title">malmstrom67 2</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Malmstrom AFB Missile/UFO Incident, Mar 16, 1967November 27, 1996...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: November 27, 1996</span></span></span>

Electromagnetic effects remain a credible engineering concern because militaries themselves acknowledge that electronic systems must be protected against hostile or accidental electromagnetic environments. However, publicly available evidence has not established that UAP caused nuclear missile failures through electromagnetic means. Reviews of UAP-related claims have not found confirmed evidence that [unidentified objects]({{ 'unidentified-objects/' | relative_url }}) represent technology capable of interfering with nuclear systems.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.dhs.gov/science-and-technology/electromagnetic-pulse-empgeomagnetic-disturbance" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: dhs.gov">[Department of Homeland Security]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">dhs.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Department of Homeland SecurityElectromagnetic Pulse (EMP)/Geomagnetic Disturbance &#124; Homeland SecuritySeptember 30, 2025...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: September 30, 2025</span></span></span>

The lasting significance of electromagnetic explanations is therefore not that they prove a mysterious cause, but that they show how dependent nuclear missile systems are on electronic resilience. A missile can remain physically intact while becoming unavailable because of a failure in the invisible electrical systems that support it. That engineering reality explains why electromagnetic protection has been a central concern in missile design and why unexplained failures naturally attract attention when they occur near nuclear facilities.
