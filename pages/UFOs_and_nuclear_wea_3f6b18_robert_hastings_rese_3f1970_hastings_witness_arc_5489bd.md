## Introduction

Robert Hastings built his nuclear UFO research around a large archive of interviews with former military personnel who said they witnessed unusual events near [nuclear weapons]({{ 'nuclear-weapons/' | relative_url }}) facilities. His approach does not rest on one incident alone; it depends on the accumulation of accounts from missile officers, security personnel, maintenance workers, and other Air Force veterans who described encounters at sites connected with nuclear weapons. Hastings has stated that he interviewed more than 150 military witnesses over several decades as part of his investigation into reports involving missile bases, weapons storage areas, and related facilities.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ufohastings.com/about" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ufohastings.com">[UFO Hastings]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ufohastings.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">UFO Hastings UFOs &amp; Nukes</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UFO Hastings UFOs &amp; Nukes</span></span></span>

## Former military witnesses interviewed

### Why personnel testimony became the foundation

Hastings’ interviewing strategy focused on people who had direct roles around nuclear systems rather than casual observers. His archive includes former launch officers, missile maintenance personnel, security police, and commanders who reported unusual aerial activity or unexplained incidents while assigned to nuclear-related duties. Hastings argues that these witnesses were particularly valuable because their training required them to recognise normal military operations, equipment status, and security procedures.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ufohastings.com/about" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ufohastings.com">[UFO Hastings]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ufohastings.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">UFO Hastings UFOs &amp; Nukes</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UFO Hastings UFOs &amp; Nukes</span></span></span>

The most prominent examples come from Cold War-era missile facilities. Former Air Force personnel connected to sites such as Malmstrom Air Force Base in Montana described incidents in which missile systems were reportedly affected during periods when unusual objects were observed nearby. Former launch officer [Robert Salas]({{ 'robert-salas/' | relative_url }}) became one of the best-known witnesses associated with claims that missiles went offline during a 1967 incident. Hastings incorporated such accounts into his argument that nuclear facilities repeatedly appeared in reports of unexplained aerial activity.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ufohastings.com/articles/the-ufos-nukes-connection-press-conference" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ufohastings.com">[UFO Hastings]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ufohastings.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">UFO Hastings UFOs &amp; Nukes</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UFO Hastings UFOs &amp; Nukes</span></span></span>

Hastings also presented witnesses publicly, most notably during the 2010 Washington, DC, press conference known as “UFOs and Nukes”. The event featured retired Air Force personnel who discussed their reported experiences and provided affidavits describing incidents connected with nuclear weapons sites. The use of sworn statements was intended to show that witnesses were willing to attach their names and reputations to their accounts rather than remain anonymous.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ufohastings.com/articles/the-ufos-nukes-connection-press-conference" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ufohastings.com">[UFO Hastings]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ufohastings.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">UFO Hastings UFOs &amp; Nukes</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UFO Hastings UFOs &amp; Nukes</span></span></span>

## Why personnel testimony matters


<div class="content-callout content-callout--key content-callout--takeaway" markdown="1">

The appeal of Hastings’ witness archive is that many witnesses had professional responsibilities that appear, at first glance, to make their testimony more significant than ordinary UFO reports. Missile officers and security personnel worked in environments where unusual events could have national security implications, and their accounts often describe specific locations, procedures, and chains of command rather than vague sightings.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ufohastings.com/about" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ufohastings.com">[UFO Hastings]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ufohastings.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">UFO Hastings UFOs &amp; Nukes</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UFO Hastings UFOs &amp; Nukes</span></span></span>

</div>

For supporters of Hastings’ research, the repeated appearance of similar themes across different witnesses is one of the strongest arguments. They point to recurring descriptions of unusual objects near nuclear installations, security concerns, and alleged technical effects involving missile systems. Hastings has argued that patterns across independent interviews are more important than any single testimony because they suggest a broader phenomenon rather than isolated mistakes or misunderstandings.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.ufohastings.com/interviews" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ufohastings.com">[UFO Hastings]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ufohastings.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">UFO Hastings UFOs &amp; Nukes</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UFO Hastings UFOs &amp; Nukes</span></span></span>

However, witness importance does not automatically settle the question of cause. A trained military witness may provide reliable information about what they saw, what alarms occurred, or what procedures followed, while the explanation for those events remains uncertain. A person can be a credible observer of an unexplained event without necessarily being able to identify its source. This distinction is a major part of the debate over Hastings’ methodology.

The Pentagon’s All-domain Anomaly Resolution Office (AARO) examined several historical claims involving former missile personnel from bases including Malmstrom, Ellsworth, Vandenberg, and Minot. Its historical review noted that it interviewed former Air Force members who described UAP sightings and alleged disruptions to intercontinental ballistic missile operations, but it did not find evidence confirming that [extraterrestrial]({{ 'extraterrestrial/' | relative_url }}) technology caused these events.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://uap-archive.org/uap/records/aaro-historical-record-report-vol1/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: uap-archive.org">[UAP Records Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">uap-archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on uap-archive.org.</span></span></span>

## Limits of retrospective accounts

### Memory, documentation and missing records

A major challenge for Hastings’ witness archive is that many interviews were conducted decades after the reported incidents. Retrospective testimony can preserve important information, particularly when witnesses describe events that were not publicly discussed at the time, but memory can also change through later discussion, repeated retelling, or exposure to other interpretations. This issue applies to any historical investigation based heavily on personal recollection.

The Malmstrom missile cases illustrate this difficulty. Witnesses have described [missile failures]({{ 'nuclear-missile/' | relative_url }}) occurring alongside reports of unusual objects, and Hastings has treated these accounts as key evidence. At the same time, establishing a direct link between the sighting and the technical malfunction requires more than testimony alone. It requires records showing exactly what happened to the equipment, what alternative explanations were considered, and whether an external cause can be demonstrated.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://uap-archive.org/uap/records/aaro-historical-record-report-vol1/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: uap-archive.org">[UAP Records Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">uap-archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on uap-archive.org.</span></span></span>

AARO’s review highlights this distinction by separating the existence of witness claims from proof of the claims’ interpretation. The office acknowledged interviewing former missile personnel and examining related allegations, while concluding that available evidence did not demonstrate that UAP represented extraterrestrial technology or caused the reported effects.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://uap-archive.org/uap/records/aaro-historical-record-report-vol1/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: uap-archive.org">[UAP Records Archive]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">uap-archive.org</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Open source on uap-archive.org.</span></span></span>

### The difference between credible witnesses and proven conclusions

The central credibility issue is not simply whether Hastings’ witnesses are honest. Many supporters and critics agree that former military personnel can sincerely report unusual experiences. The disagreement concerns what conclusions can reasonably be drawn from those reports.

Hastings’ strongest contribution is the preservation of a substantial body of testimony from people who worked around nuclear weapons systems. His interviews provide a historical record of how certain military veterans interpreted unusual incidents during and after the Cold War. The more difficult question is whether those interpretations demonstrate an unknown technological cause, a classified human activity, environmental explanations, equipment failures, or other possibilities.

This distinction explains why Hastings’ research remains influential but contested. His witness archive expanded public discussion of nuclear-related UFO reports by bringing forward former personnel who might otherwise never have spoken publicly. Yet the evidential weight of the archive depends on how readers assess the gap between a documented personal account and a verified explanation for the event described.
