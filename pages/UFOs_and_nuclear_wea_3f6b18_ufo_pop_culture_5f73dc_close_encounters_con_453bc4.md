## Introduction

*Close Encounters of the Third Kind* (1977) changed the popular image of UFOs by presenting alien contact as a question of communication, curiosity, and human transformation rather than simply invasion or destruction. Directed by Steven Spielberg, the film arrived after decades of Cold War science fiction in which extraterrestrials were often symbols of threat, reflecting fears of nuclear war, technological competition, and social instability. Spielberg instead imagined a first encounter built around recognition: humans and visitors trying to understand one another across an enormous divide.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.loc.gov/item/event-421188/close-encounters-of-the-third-kind-1977/2026-06-05/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: loc.gov">[The Library of Congress]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">loc.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Library of CongressClose Encounters of the Third Kind (1977)* &#124; Library of CongressJune 5, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 5, 2026</span></span></span>

The film became one of the most influential cultural reference points for modern ideas about [extraterrestrial]({{ 'extraterrestrial/' | relative_url }}) contact. It did not provide evidence that aliens exist, but it shaped how many people imagine what contact might look like: a mysterious arrival, a search for meaning, and a moment requiring cooperation rather than military response. Its importance within UFO popular culture lies in how it moved the alien from a symbol of danger towards a possible partner in communication.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.smithsonianmag.com/science-nature/ready-for-contact-76322347/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: smithsonianmag.com">[Smithsonian Magazine]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">smithsonianmag.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">Smithsonian Magazine Ready for Contact</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Smithsonian MagazineReady for ContactDecember 1, 2010...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: December 1, 2010</span></span></span>

## Cold War alien invasion films

Before *Close Encounters*, many major UFO films reflected the anxieties of the atomic age. During the 1950s and 1960s, science fiction frequently portrayed extraterrestrials as hostile forces that threatened human survival. Films such as *The Day the Earth Stood Still* (1951), *The War of the Worlds* (1953), and *Invasion of the Body Snatchers* (1956) used alien encounters to explore fears about invasion, ideological conflict, and the consequences of uncontrolled technology.

This pattern connected naturally with Cold War concerns. The possibility of unknown forces approaching Earth echoed worries about [nuclear weapons]({{ 'nuclear-weapons/' | relative_url }}), espionage, and sudden catastrophe. In these stories, the central question was often whether humanity could defend itself against a superior power. Alien visitors became a metaphor for external threats at a time when societies were already concerned about scientific advances that could transform or destroy civilisation.

*Close Encounters of the Third Kind* kept some of this uncertainty but changed the emotional direction. The unknown was no longer automatically an enemy. The film’s tension comes from the difficulty of understanding the visitors, not from a battle against them. The US government and military initially approach the phenomenon through secrecy and control, while ordinary people and [scientists]({{ 'scientists/' | relative_url }}) are drawn towards the possibility of dialogue. This shift helped create a new model of UFO storytelling centred on contact rather than conflict.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.loc.gov/item/event-421188/close-encounters-of-the-third-kind-1977/2026-06-05/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: loc.gov">[The Library of Congress]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">loc.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Library of CongressClose Encounters of the Third Kind (1977)* &#124; Library of CongressJune 5, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 5, 2026</span></span></span>

## Spielberg's communication-focused vision

### From sightings to a language of contact

One of the film’s most distinctive ideas is that communication is the true challenge of first contact. The visitors do not arrive with an obvious demand or attack; instead, they use lights, sounds, and symbols to establish a shared understanding. The famous musical exchange between humans and the extraterrestrials presents communication as a creative act rather than a military negotiation.

The title itself comes from the classification system developed by astronomer J. Allen [Hynek]({{ 'hynek/' | relative_url }}), who worked as a scientific consultant on the film. Hynek’s close-encounter categories were originally part of his attempt to organise reports of UFO experiences, with a “third kind” encounter involving observation of occupants associated with a UFO. Spielberg adapted this idea into a story about direct interaction between humans and another intelligence.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.smithsonianmag.com/history/whats-behind-steven-spielbergs-lifelong-obsession-with-flying-saucers-and-extraterrestrial-visitors-180988903/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: smithsonianmag.com">[Smithsonian Magazine]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">smithsonianmag.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Smithsonian MagazineThe Alien Inspiration Behind Steven Spielberg&#x27;s &#x27;Disclosure Day&#x27;June 11, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 11, 2026</span></span></span>

The involvement of Hynek also shows the film’s unusual position between science and popular imagination. It borrowed terminology from UFO research but used it for a fictional exploration of human emotions, belief, and uncertainty. The result was not a scientific argument for alien visitation; it was a cultural thought experiment about how people might react if humanity encountered something genuinely unknown.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.smithsonianmag.com/history/whats-behind-steven-spielbergs-lifelong-obsession-with-flying-saucers-and-extraterrestrial-visitors-180988903/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: smithsonianmag.com">[Smithsonian Magazine]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">smithsonianmag.com</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Smithsonian MagazineThe Alien Inspiration Behind Steven Spielberg&#x27;s &#x27;Disclosure Day&#x27;June 11, 2026...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: June 11, 2026</span></span></span>

### Ordinary people at the centre of the mystery

Another major change was the choice of protagonists. Rather than focusing only on astronauts, generals, or scientists, the film follows ordinary people, especially Roy Neary, an electrical worker whose encounter changes the direction of his life. His obsession with a mysterious shape and his search for meaning make the alien experience personal rather than purely technological.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.bbfc.co.uk/release/close-encounters-of-the-third-kind-q29sbgvjdglvbjpwwc0yodkznta" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: bbfc.co.uk">[BBFC]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">bbfc.co.uk</span><span class="citation-popover-title">BBFCBBFC &#124; BBFC</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">BBFCBBFC &#124; BBFC</span></span></span>

This focus influenced later alien-contact stories by suggesting that first contact would not only be a government event or scientific discovery. It could also be a psychological and social experience affecting individuals, families, and communities. The film’s enduring appeal comes partly from this tension: the arrival of extraterrestrials is a global event, but the story is experienced through very human emotions such as wonder, confusion, and longing.

## Changing images of extraterrestrial visitors

*Close Encounters of the Third Kind* helped replace the image of the threatening alien with a more complex visitor. The extraterrestrials remain mysterious and powerful, but their purpose is not conquest. Their arrival represents a possibility of connection, challenging the assumption that an advanced civilisation would necessarily view humanity as an enemy.

This idea influenced later science fiction, including Spielberg’s own *E.T. the Extra-Terrestrial* (1982), which further developed the theme of friendship and emotional connection between humans and alien beings. Together, these films contributed to a broader cultural shift in which extraterrestrials could represent empathy, discovery, and questions about humanity’s place in the universe.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://ew.com/steven-spielberg-and-aliens-tracing-a-decades-long-obsession-11995978" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: ew.com">[EW.com]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">ew.com</span><span class="citation-popover-title">steven spielberg and aliens tracing a decades long obsession 11995978</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">His 2026 return with “Disclosure Day” reflects both his earliest and most apocalyptic tendencies—merging curiosity with an urgent questio...</span></span></span>

## Why the film remains central to UFO culture

The lasting influence of *Close Encounters of the Third Kind* comes from its unusual answer to a classic UFO question: what would happen if the unknown arrived? Earlier stories often answered with fear. Spielberg answered with a search for understanding.

Within UFO mythology, especially stories connected to secrecy, technology, and the nuclear age, the film offered an alternative vision. Instead of imagining advanced beings as a warning about humanity’s destruction, it imagined contact as a test of humanity’s ability to listen and cooperate. That idea helped make UFO stories about more than unexplained objects in the sky; they became stories about communication, identity, and humanity’s relationship with the wider universe.

The film’s place in cultural history is also recognised institutionally: *Close Encounters of the Third Kind* was selected for preservation in the US National Film Registry, reflecting its significance as a work of American cinema.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.loc.gov/programs/national-film-preservation-board/film-registry/complete-national-film-registry-listing/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: loc.gov">[The Library of Congress]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">loc.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">The Library of CongressComplete National Film Registry Listing &#124; Film Registry &#124; National Film Preservation Board &#124; Programs &#124; Library of...</span></span></span> Its influence endures because it transformed the alien encounter from a simple confrontation into a dramatic question about whether different forms of intelligence could find common ground.
