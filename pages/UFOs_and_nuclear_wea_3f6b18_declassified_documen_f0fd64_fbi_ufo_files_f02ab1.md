## Introduction

The FBI’s released UFO files are best understood as a record of how a major intelligence and law-enforcement agency handled reports during the early Cold War, not as a hidden archive proving extraordinary claims. The documents show that the FBI received UFO reports, exchanged information with military authorities, and preserved unusual accounts at a time when [unidentified objects]({{ 'unidentified-objects/' | relative_url }}) were considered potential air-security concerns. They also show the limits of the evidence: many files contain unverified witness statements, rumours, or administrative records rather than confirmed findings.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://vault.fbi.gov/search?SearchableText=ufo" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: vault.fbi.gov">[FBI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">vault.fbi.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">— Federal Bureau of Investigation</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">FBI — Federal Bureau of Investigation...</span></span></span>

The FBI collection matters because it adds an intelligence-transparency layer to the wider [declassified UFO]({{ 'declassified-ufo/' | relative_url }}) archive. While Air Force programmes such as [Project Blue Book]({{ 'project-blue-book/' | relative_url }}) focused on investigating sightings, FBI files reveal how reports moved through government channels, how officials decided what deserved attention, and how later Freedom of Information Act (FOIA) releases changed public access to once-obscure records.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://vault.fbi.gov/search?SearchableText=ufo" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: vault.fbi.gov">[FBI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">vault.fbi.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">— Federal Bureau of Investigation</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">FBI — Federal Bureau of Investigation...</span></span></span>

## How intelligence agencies received reports

During the late 1940s and early 1950s, UFO reports appeared during a period of intense national security anxiety. The newly established US Air Force became the main organisation responsible for investigating sightings, but the FBI also received reports and worked with military authorities for a period. The FBI’s own released description of its UFO collection states that the files cover its role in investigating reports between 1947 and 1954, when concerns about unknown aircraft overlapped with wider Cold War intelligence priorities.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://vault.fbi.gov/search?SearchableText=ufo" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: vault.fbi.gov">[FBI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">vault.fbi.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">— Federal Bureau of Investigation</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">FBI — Federal Bureau of Investigation...</span></span></span>

The FBI’s role was narrower than popular accounts often suggest. It was not the primary scientific body analysing UFO cases, and its files do not represent a parallel equivalent of Air Force investigations. Instead, FBI documents often show information collection: field offices forwarded reports, agents recorded claims, and communications were passed to relevant military organisations. The surviving records illustrate a government responding to uncertainty rather than a single agency conducting a secret programme of discovery.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://vault.fbi.gov/search?SearchableText=ufo" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: vault.fbi.gov">[FBI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">vault.fbi.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">— Federal Bureau of Investigation</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">FBI — Federal Bureau of Investigation...</span></span></span>

### The 1947–1954 reporting pipeline

The early files show a chain of information movement:


<div class="content-enhancement content-enhancement--insight-grid" markdown="1">

* members of the public, pilots, police officers, and military personnel reported unusual sightings;
* FBI field offices sometimes received or recorded those reports;
* military organisations, particularly the Air Force, handled technical assessment;
* intelligence concerns influenced whether an event was treated as a possible security issue.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://vault.fbi.gov/search?SearchableText=ufo" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: vault.fbi.gov">[FBI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">vault.fbi.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">— Federal Bureau of Investigation</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">FBI — Federal Bureau of Investigation...</span></span></span>

</div>

This process is important for understanding UFO records connected with sensitive sites, including locations linked to aviation, weapons development, and Cold War defence. A report appearing in FBI files does not mean the Bureau verified the event; it often means the claim entered an official information system and was preserved.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://vault.fbi.gov/search?SearchableText=ufos" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: vault.fbi.gov">[FBI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">vault.fbi.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">— Federal Bureau of Investigation</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">FBI — Federal Bureau of Investigation...</span></span></span>

## FOIA releases and document trails

The public understanding of FBI UFO records changed significantly through FOIA and digital archives. The FBI Vault, the Bureau’s online repository for released records, provides public access to documents processed through FOIA and other disclosure mechanisms. The archive was designed to make previously difficult-to-access FBI records available for public review.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://vault.fbi.gov/foia" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: vault.fbi.gov">[FBI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">vault.fbi.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">— Federal Bureau of Investigation</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">FBI — Federal Bureau of Investigation...</span></span></span>

The UFO files became particularly prominent because they combined two powerful public interests: government [transparency]({{ 'transparency/' | relative_url }}) and unexplained events. However, the release process also demonstrated why document availability and evidential strength are different questions. A government file can prove that officials received a report without proving that the reported event occurred as described.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.fbi.gov/news/stories/ufos-and-the-guy-hottel-memo" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: fbi.gov">[FBI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">fbi.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ufos and the guy hottel memo</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UFOs and the Guy Hottel Memo — FBIMarch 25, 2013...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 25, 2013</span></span></span>

### The Guy Hottel memo and the problem of interpretation

The most famous FBI UFO document is the March 22, 1950 memo from Guy Hottel, then special agent in charge of the FBI’s Washington Field Office. The memo recorded a claim passed through several people alleging that recovered “flying saucers” and occupants had been found in New Mexico. Because of its dramatic content, the document attracted enormous public attention after being highlighted in the FBI Vault.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.fbi.gov/image-repository/history/hottel-memo.jpeg/view" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: fbi.gov">[FBI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">fbi.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">Guy Hottel Memo, 1950 — FBI...</span></span></span>

The FBI’s own explanation of the memo is more cautious. The Bureau states that the document was a second- or third-hand report that was never investigated by the FBI and does not prove the existence of UFOs or recovered craft. The memo is therefore valuable as a historical document showing what information reached officials, but it is not direct evidence confirming the claim described inside it.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://www.fbi.gov/news/stories/ufos-and-the-guy-hottel-memo" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: fbi.gov">[FBI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">fbi.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">ufos and the guy hottel memo</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">UFOs and the Guy Hottel Memo — FBIMarch 25, 2013...</span><span class="citation-popover-meta">Published: March 25, 2013</span></span></span>

This distinction is central to intelligence transparency research: archives preserve the decisions, rumours, leads, and uncertainties that governments recorded. They do not automatically transform every recorded allegation into a verified event.

## Secrecy versus evidence

The FBI UFO files highlight a recurring tension in government transparency: secrecy can result from genuine national-security concerns, but secrecy can also fuel public suspicion when records are unavailable or poorly explained.

During the early Cold War, unexplained aerial reports were handled in an environment where foreign aircraft, surveillance technology, experimental systems, and military vulnerabilities were serious concerns. A report near a sensitive facility could attract official attention because of possible security implications even when the final explanation was ordinary or remained uncertain.<span class="citation-link-wrap"><a class="citation-inline-link" href="https://vault.fbi.gov/search?SearchableText=ufo" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer nofollow" aria-label="View source: vault.fbi.gov">[FBI]</a><span class="citation-popover" role="note"><span class="citation-popover-source">vault.fbi.gov</span><span class="citation-popover-title">— Federal Bureau of Investigation</span><span class="citation-popover-snippet">FBI — Federal Bureau of Investigation...</span></span></span>

The lasting importance of the FBI archive is not that it resolves every UFO claim. Its value is that it reveals the machinery behind government responses:
